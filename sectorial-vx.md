Your approach is strategically sound, and I think it could become a valuable differentiator in how cyber risk is incorporated into corporate credit decisions.

However, I would make one important refinement to your proposition:

The questionnaire identifies a potential weakness. The sector intelligence report establishes why that weakness matters in the client's industry, how attackers have exploited it, and what the consequences could be for the client's financial resilience.

The ultimate objective is not to determine whether a borrower has good cybersecurity. It is to understand whether weaknesses in its cybersecurity could materially affect its ability to operate, generate cash and service debt.

## 2. What I would recommend the sector report contain

I would call it a Sector Cyber Resilience & Credit Intelligence Report.

It should be a decision-support instrument, not a conventional threat intelligence report.

My preferred structure:

| Component                                  | What it must deliver                                                                                                               | Priority  |
| ------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------- |
| 1. Sector executive assessment             | A concise judgement of the sector's exposure, operational dependence on technology, resilience and potential financial sensitivity | Essential |
| 2. Sector operating model and dependencies | What digital systems, OT, supply chains or critical providers keep the sector functioning; where single points of failure exist    | Essential |
| 3. Dominant attack scenarios               | The 3–5 most credible attack pathways, supported by recent incident evidence, rather than a catalogue of cyber threats             | Essential |
| 4. Control weakness intelligence           | Which missing controls have enabled attacks, the circumstances in which they matter most and evidence of the consequences          | Essential |
| 5. Incident evidence and lessons           | Selected real-world cases showing initial weakness, attack mechanism, business disruption and financial outcomes                   | Essential |
| 6. Credit impact and stress scenarios      | How disruption translates into lost revenue, cash-flow pressure, recovery costs, liquidity needs and debt-servicing capacity       | Essential |
| 7. Sector resilience priorities            | The handful of safeguards and recovery capabilities with the greatest risk-reduction value for that industry                       | Essential |
| 8. Credit officer discussion guide         | Questions and suggested follow-ups linked directly to the questionnaire responses and sector risks                                 | Essential |

The most valuable components are 4, 5 and 6. Those turn intelligence into something credit officers can actually use.

## 3. The centrepiece: evidence-based control weakness intelligence

This should be the distinguishing feature of your reports.

Every important questionnaire control needs a corresponding sector intelligence explanation.

ILLUSTRATIVE INTELLIGENCE ENTRY · HEALTHCARE

## Risk scenario: Compromised remote-access credentials

Client questionnaire finding

MFA not fully implemented

Documented incident

In February 2024, attackers used compromised credentials to access a Change Healthcare remote-access portal that lacked MFA. They subsequently moved laterally, stole data and deployed ransomware nine days after initial access. The attack disrupted healthcare claims and payment processing. This was confirmed in UnitedHealth Group's congressional testimony.&#x20;

Sector-specific significance

Healthcare organisations depend on the availability of patient, billing and payment systems. An identity-control failure affecting critical infrastructure can therefore propagate into revenue collection and liquidity disruption, including for organisations that were not directly compromised.

Credit transmission

Weak remote-access authentication

Compromise and service disruption

Delayed billing and collections

Liquidity and debt-service pressure

Questions for the credit officer

- Does the MFA gap include remote access or privileged accounts?
- How long could your organisation continue operating if billing or claims systems became unavailable?
- What alternative payment or processing arrangements have been tested?

Recommended client improvement: Prioritise MFA for critical access, and demonstrate continuity arrangements for essential revenue-generating services.

This demonstrates an essential principle: a control gap is not automatically a material credit risk. The exposure, operational dependency, compensating controls and financial capacity of the particular borrower determine materiality.

## 4. Make financial resilience the differentiator

Most threat intelligence reports stop at the incident. Yours should continue to the credit consequences.

I would introduce three analytical dimensions for each major sector scenario:

Threat exposure

How plausible is this attack pathway in the sector, based on attack surface, attacker activity and observed incidents?

Operational sensitivity

What critical functions could stop, and how dependent is the business model on their uninterrupted availability?

Financial absorption capacity

Can the borrower withstand the disruption through cash reserves, alternative operations, insurance and available liquidity?

The report can establish the first two at sector level. The credit officer uses borrower-specific information to address the third.

For example, an eight-week disruption might be manageable for a well-capitalised company with alternative production facilities, but critical for a highly leveraged single-site manufacturer.

I would avoid turning these dimensions into a purportedly precise probability of default until you have validated data and an approved credit methodology.

## 5. Four design decisions I would insist on

First — Segment by business model, not just sector. A pharmaceutical manufacturer, a hospital and a healthcare payment processor do not have the same cyber-to-credit risk transmission. Sector classifications alone are too coarse. Use sector, subsector, geography and operating dependencies.

Second — Make the questionnaire and intelligence report interoperable. Each questionnaire item should map to relevant sector scenarios, documented incidents, financial impact channels and discussion prompts. Credit officers should not have to discover those connections themselves.

Third — Distinguish evidence from judgement. For every major claim, record the source, incident date, whether a control failure was confirmed or merely suspected, and analytical confidence. Incident frequency in a published dataset is not necessarily the probability of a borrower suffering an attack.

Fourth — Keep it proportionate. The purpose is structured dialogue, not formal assurance. Self-reported responses must not be presented as independently verified control effectiveness. Significant concerns can justify a mitigation discussion, closer monitoring or specialist referral, not an automatic adverse credit conclusion.

## 6. How I would package the product

I would keep the main report to four pages maximum, with a maintained evidence annex behind it.

Page 1 — Sector risk briefing

Executive assessment, top three risks, operating dependencies and major credit implications. Readable in two minutes.

Page 2 — Attack scenarios and control-gap evidence

Attack pathways mapped to questionnaire items, supported by documented sector incidents.

Page 3 — Financial and operational resilience

Business interruption pathways, exposure concentrations, plausible stress scenarios and critical recovery capabilities.

Page 4 — Credit officer engagement playbook

Prioritised discussion prompts, practical improvement recommendations and circumstances requiring escalation.

The evidence annex should be continuously maintainable, with quarterly report reviews and exceptional updates when major incidents materially change the sector outlook.

## 7. My proposed operating principle

There are three separate products in your operating model:

Questionnaire

What weaknesses or resilience gaps may exist at the borrower?

Sector Cyber Intelligence

Why do those weaknesses matter, what has happened elsewhere, and what consequences are credible?

Credit Risk Interpretation

How financially material could the exposure be for this particular borrower, and what follow-up is proportionate?

I would not merge all three prematurely into one cyber score. They answer different questions.

## 8. Where I would start

I would pilot this with one sector — UK retail or manufacturing — because both allow you to connect cyber incidents to tangible business interruption, revenue losses and supply-chain dependencies. There is also usable sector-specific research, including S&P's analysis of UK retail incidents and Verizon's manufacturing reporting.&#x20;

Build the initial report around five or six critical questionnaire weaknesses, and test whether credit officers can use it to hold better conversations without requiring cybersecurity expertise.

The success measure should not be the number of cyber risks identified. It should be whether the intelligence produces more informed client discussions, credible mitigation commitments and better identification of potentially material credit exposures.

My central recommendation is to design this as a cyber-to-credit intelligence capability, rather than a collection of sectorial cybersecurity reports. That is where its institutional value lies.
