Movement I · Plates 02–03

Where the sector is exposed

Plate02

Four exposures, all structurally high

Banking holds transferable money, runs on shared infrastructure and answers to several authorities at once. That makes its inherent exposure high on every dimension below. What remains after a borrower's own controls is not something a sector report can tell you.

Fraud

Money that moves on instruction

Inherent: high

Banks execute payments, lend against documents and delegate authority to staff and intermediaries. A bank can be the victim, the conduit or the party that must reimburse.

£1.17bn

gross UK payment-fraud losses in 2024 across 3.31m cases. Value fell 2.7% since 2020 while cases rose 8.1%. UK Finance

Credit reading. Recurrent small losses, plus rare concentrated ones from insider, collateral or reporting fraud that payment statistics never show.

Cyber / ICT

Records that must stay true

Inherent: high

Every service depends on continuously available systems and trustworthy balances. A disruption can stop payments or settlement with no data stolen at all. Observed maturity is uneven.

65%

of 592 financial-services respondents reported ransomware in the prior year, against 59% across sectors. Broader than banks. Sophos 2024

Credit reading. Outage and data-integrity risk first, then a long tail of remediation, legal and regulatory cost.

Operational resilience / third parties

Shared pipes, shared failures

Inherent: high

Core processors, cloud, messaging and clearing are common to many banks. One supplier incident can remove the same function from several institutions on the same day.

27%

of incidents UK firms reported for 2025 came from a third party, and 37% of those were cyber-related. All financial firms, not banks alone. FCA, July 2026

Credit reading. Liquidity can tighten before any loss is booked: settlement queues, trapped collateral, replacement funding.

Governance / operating model

Accountable, but not always in control

High for complex groups · variable for simple ones

A payment may cross a local bank, a group technology company, a processor and a correspondent. The entity answerable to the customer may not direct any of them.

>10%

of contracts covering critical functions were found non-compliant in ECB supervisory work. ECB priorities 2025–27

Credit reading. Slow decisions in a crisis, repeat findings, sustained remediation spend and possible limits on activity.

Reading

Aggregate strength is a starting point. It does not establish that a given counterparty can absorb an eight-week disruption. Recurring earnings, deposit stability, capital headroom and operational recoverability decide that.

Inherent exposure describes the sector's structure. Residual exposure depends on a borrower's tested controls, suppliers and financial buffers, and is left undetermined here. Banking is highly exposed, but the evidence does not support calling it the most exposed sector: healthcare reports higher breach costs, and energy and manufacturing carry physical-process risk that banking does not.

Shaded layers are needed to complete a transaction or to restore safely. The chain shown is the retail and commercial pattern; the dependency categories are documented for the sector, and no vendor relationship is assigned to any individual bank.

Seven places a bank can stop

  1. Records and their recovery chain. If ledger, journals and backups sit inside one compromised administrative boundary, spare capacity cannot restore trustworthy balances.
  2. Shared identity and management tools. Production, backup administration, supplier access and crisis communications go dark together.
  3. A bundled core provider. One incident removes account servicing, digital channels, payments and support at once.
  4. A sole settlement intermediary. The bank runs, but cannot complete transactions in a currency or market.
  5. Common software at both sites. An update or exploit defeats geographic redundancy.
  6. A handful of specialists. Recovery waits on a few engineers, treasury authorisers or vendor staff.
  7. Backlog capacity. Manual fallbacks keep a minimum service but cannot clear queues before settlement, margin or payroll deadlines.

Documented The dependency is real even without an attack. On 18 July 2024 a Swift Y-Copy issue stopped CHAPS settlement for 245 minutes; hours were extended and every payment settled. The 2023 ransomware attack on ION forced derivatives firms into manual processing for weeks. The strongest continuity evidence is a tested completion of the business service with trustworthy data, not the existence of a second data centre.

Movement II · Plates 04–07

What the evidence shows

Plate04

Five threats, ranked by what they can stop

The common thread is trusted access. A valid login, an administrator account, an authenticated payment message or a supplier connection carries the attacker past the perimeter. The order reflects operational severity, breadth and strength of evidence. It is not a probability ranking.

1

Privileged compromise leading to ransomware or untrustworthy data

CriticalAll banks, especially groups on shared infrastructure

Seen in. River Financial, June 2026: access around 16 June, detected 19 June, ransomware across part of the server estate, administrator accounts disabled, systems taken offline, data removed. How the attackers first got in was not disclosed.

What it does to the business. Accounts and servicing unavailable, payments delayed, positions uncertain, long manual reconciliation. The bank may be unable to show that balances are correct.

stolen credentials or manipulated account reset → administrator rights → directories, virtualisation, backups → data theft and encryption → isolation and reconciliation

2

Critical supplier compromise affecting many institutions at once

Critical for processingHigh for dataRegional banks, BaaS, custody, derivatives

Seen in. ION, January 2023: LockBit disabled a trade-processing service used by 16 of about 60 US futures commission merchants. Marquis, SitusAMC and DriveWealth, 2025–26: customer data taken from suppliers while the banks' own networks were untouched.

What it does to the business. Clearing and settlement backlogs, or correlated notification and litigation exposure. Ending a supplier relationship does not end exposure: DriveWealth held records of former clients.

supplier intrusion → hosted service unavailable, or retained data stolen → the same loss lands on every client

3

Identity and process deception that walks data out

HighDigital banks, consumer lenders, wealth and private banking

Seen in. Revolut, disclosed 12 September 2026: an email impersonating a government agency induced staff to release identity documents and transaction records. No system was hacked. Capital One, 2019: a misconfigured firewall exposed stored data.

What it does to the business. KYC, credit and wealth records exposed; customer-protection costs, litigation and follow-on scams. Core banking keeps running throughout, which is why it is easy to underrate.

phishing, voice deception or a forged official request → valid access or voluntary disclosure → bulk extraction → extortion or targeted fraud

4

Payment manipulation, account takeover and ATM cash-out

High to criticalRetail, commercial and transaction banks, ATM operators

Seen in. Bangladesh Bank, 2016: fraudulent instructions sent from the bank's own payment terminals. US ATM jackpotting: physical access plus Ploutus malware forcing machines to dispense cash.

What it does to the business. Direct theft, recalls, suspended channels, customer disputes. A successful cash-out needs no bank-wide outage.

compromised endpoint or customer → instruction that looks authorised → funds dispersed across borders before recall

5

DDoS and coordinated disruption of customer access

HighDigital banks, payments, API-connected businesses

Seen in. Vendor telemetry ranks financial services as the most targeted industry for network-layer DDoS, with pro-Iran and pro-Russia hacktivists naming banks and payment APIs.

What it does to the business. Login and payment failures, support overload, missed processing windows if prolonged. It becomes critical when it coincides with another failure.

botnet traffic → network, API, DNS or authentication capacity exhausted → queues and degraded service

Geopolitics chooses the target and the purpose

State-linked activity against banks comes in four forms: reading, disrupting, stealing and quietly waiting. A borrower's exposure follows its relationships, its staff and its shared infrastructure more than its nationality.

Signals that would change the assessment

  • A campaign that names banks or specific financing relationships, confirmed by telemetry or a victim rather than by the actor's own posts.
  • Phishing themed on financing, sanctions, conferences or payment advice reaching the borrower's staff.
  • Persistent access found inside a critical supplier or management platform.
  • DDoS that persists across channels or defeats failover.
  • Technical confirmation of destructive commands, or interference with backups and identity systems.
  • Settlement queues, failed reconciliations or counterparties restricting limits.

The most material escalation would be confirmed state-linked access to a shared processor, payment infrastructure or recovery provider. Independently tested containment and recovery would lower the assessed severity.

Credit reading. Banks in different countries can share one identity platform, processor or recovery provider, so concentration can outweigh geographic diversification. Conflict can also remove recovery options that were tested in peacetime: connectivity, supplier staff, travel. Attribution matters to sanctions, insurance and legal treatment and is usually unknown while the crisis is live, so expected insurance recovery should not be counted as available cash.

Plate06

The record: fourteen incidents, three mechanisms

Consequential bank incidents fall into three kinds, and each reaches credit by a different road. Stolen funds, remediation spend, settlements, penalties and temporary settlement exposures are different things and should never be added into one loss figure.

Payment integrity. Legitimate infrastructure executes fraudulent instructionsConfidentiality. Data stolen, service continuesAvailability and supply chain. Processing impaired

Landmarks

  1. 2014JPMorgan Chase. Contact data for 76m households and 7m small businesses taken. No account numbers or passwords, and no related fraud reported at the time.
  2. Feb 2016Bangladesh Bank. $81m transferred to Philippine accounts through the bank's own SWIFT terminals; a further $20m stopped. SWIFT's central network was not breached.
  3. Mar 2019Capital One. About 100m US and 6m Canadian individuals affected through a misconfigured firewall. $80m regulatory penalty in 2020, $190m customer settlement later.
  4. Jan 2023ION Cleared Derivatives. Ransomware at a supplier. Trade processing, margin and reporting ran manually; most services back by 19 February. No defaults.
  5. Nov 2023ICBC Financial Services. Ransomware disrupted US Treasury clearing and left a temporary $9bn obligation to its settlement bank. A funding need, not a loss.

Twelve months to 27 October 2026

  1. 2025US ATM jackpotting. 700+ incidents and $20m+ in losses reported by the FBI for the year; sanctions and prosecutions followed in autumn 2026.
  2. Aug 2025Marquis. Data-services supplier to banks and credit unions hit by ransomware through a firewall. 672,075 people in one state filing alone.
  3. Nov 2025SitusAMC. Mortgage-services supplier; personal data acquired over a week. Client banks' networks not shown to be breached.
  4. Mar 2026Standard Bank. Administrative and document systems accessed. Core and transaction systems stayed available.
  5. Jun 2026River Financial. Ransomware and data removal at a US regional bank. Financial impact still unresolved in its July filing.
  6. Jun 2026Melli, Tejarat, Saderat, EDBI. Four Iranian banks disrupted through one shared communications layer.
  7. Sep 2026Revolut. Customer data released to someone impersonating a government agency.
  8. Sep 2026DriveWealth. Brokerage supplier breached by social engineering; records of former clients' customers exposed.
  9. Oct 2026South Korean cluster. The regulator confirmed incidents at 16 financial firms and about 100,000 exposed records, including Shinhan, KB Kookmin, Hana and BNK Busan. One bank's count grew from 119 to 2,210 people within a month.

Three cases, three lessons for the sector

Bangladesh Bank · payment integrity

An authenticated message is not a legitimate instruction

Attackers reached the terminals that speak to SWIFT and sent payment orders that looked genuine in every technical respect. Protecting the endpoint was necessary and not sufficient.

What independent check sits between a valid login and a large payment?

Capital One · confidentiality

Using a cloud provider does not transfer the risk

Configuration and permissions stayed the bank's responsibility. Service never stopped, yet the consequences ran for years: a penalty for weak cloud-risk assessment and slow correction, then a settlement.

Who owns configuration, and how fast are known gaps closed?

ION · availability

A healthy bank network is not enough

Client firms were not breached, but could not process trades. Manual work and clearing-house help met every margin call. The workaround existed and still could not keep normal throughput or timely reporting.

Can the fallback carry real volume, and for how long?

Entries are selected for significance and documentation, not sampled, so they cannot be turned into an incident rate. Unverified ransomware-gang claims against two further banks in 2026 are left out, as are non-cyber outages such as the March 2026 Lloyds data-display defect and the October 2026 cloud disruption. Initial access is unknown for most recent cases, and later notices about one incident are not counted as new attacks.

Six pairs that are easy to mistake for each other

MFA is not identity protectionEnrolment, reset, help-desk verification, service accounts and emergency access are where attackers go.
A backup is not a recoveryHolding copies says nothing about clean, complete and timely restoration with correct balances.
A certificate is not assuranceScope and findings matter. ISO 27001 or SOC 2 may cover a fraction of the critical services.
A plan is not a testRecovery plans are more complete than recovery evidence across the supervised population.
A contract is not an exitSupplier assurance often stops at paperwork that proves neither substitution nor joint recovery.
Sector exposure is not a borrower weaknessThe table describes what banking needs. Whether a given bank has it is established only by its own evidence.

Proportionality applies. A small bank with a well-governed outsourced core can be resilient; a large group with several data centres can still be concentrated. Multi-cloud, micro-segmentation or an in-house security operations centre are justified by risk and outcome, not presumed necessary for every institution. There is no defensible single worldwide banking maturity score.

Movement III · Plates 08–10

What is expected, and what it costs

Plate08

Three regimes, one direction

The EU has one harmonised law, the UK regulates outcomes for important services, and the US layers rules by charter and activity. All three now expect a bank to prove it can keep critical services running and to tell its supervisor quickly when it cannot.

European Union

One binding framework

  • DORA, in force since 17 January 2025: ICT risk, incident reporting, resilience testing, third-party risk and a register of dependencies.
  • Advanced threat-led testing applies to selected institutions only.
  • 19 technology providers designated as critical and overseen directly. Designation certifies nothing about a bank's own arrangement.
  • GDPR runs in parallel. A DORA report does not discharge it.
  • Third-country branches should not assume they fall outside DORA.

ECB for significant banks; national authorities otherwise; EBA sets standards.

United Kingdom

Outcomes for important services

  • Operational resilience rules: identify important business services, set impact tolerances, map dependencies, test severe but plausible scenarios. Firms have had to stay within tolerance since 31 March 2025.
  • Outsourcing rules cover materiality, audit rights, concentration and exit. Management stays accountable.
  • Designated critical third parties are overseen jointly by the Bank, PRA and FCA.
  • A unified incident-reporting framework starts 18 March 2027. Existing duties apply until then.

PRA for prudential soundness, FCA for conduct, ICO for data.

United States

Layered by charter and activity

  • GLBA-derived security standards require a risk-based programme with testing and supplier oversight.
  • The federal notification rule, SEC disclosure for listed groups, NYDFS Part 500 and state breach laws can all apply to one incident.
  • FFIEC handbooks, NIST CSF and the OCC work programme inform examiners. They are guidance, not statute.
  • CIRCIA reporting was not yet effective at the cutoff; third-party guidance changes are proposals.

OCC, Federal Reserve, FDIC or NCUA by charter, plus state and securities regulators.

Fines are not automatic and an incident does not by itself prove a breach of rules. The larger exposures are usually ordered remediation, restrictions on activity, supplier replacement and customer redress. Offsets to verifyAlternative rails, manual capacity, clean data, drawable liquidity, enforceable group support, insurance net of sublimits and waiting periods.

For deposit-taking banks, read the effect through net interest income, pre-provision profit, cost-to-income, liquidity and regulatory capital. EBITDA and net debt measures apply at holding companies, nonbank lenders and suppliers. No cyber-attributable covenant breach, debt-service default or rating downgrade is established for the cases in this document. That is a limit of the evidence, not assurance that it cannot happen, and nothing here is a conclusion about any individual borrower.

How to use it with a client

Plate11

Reading the client questionnaire

The questionnaire asks whether things exist. In banking the credit question is whether they work under stress. For each of its five themes, this plate sets out why a weak answer matters in this sector, where it has gone wrong before, and what makes it more or less significant for a particular borrower.

Before reading any answer, establish which of five things it means

Not in placeOutsourcedNot applicableNot knownIn place, not independently tested

All answers are self-reported. A "yes" is a statement to explore, not a verified control. A "no" is not a finding of non-compliance.

Theme 1

Security leadership and knowing what you own

Covers the security lead and team, their reporting line, the written programme, the asset inventory and asset classification.

Why it matters in banking
Services cross legal entities, group technology companies and suppliers. Without a named owner and a current map of what supports each service, recovery priorities are guessed during the crisis.
Where it went wrong
Capital One was penalised $80m for cloud-risk assessment failings and slow correction, a governance finding. New York's regulator in 2026 still cited incomplete asset inventories as a recurring weakness.
Road to credit
Longer outages, ordered remediation, sustained expense and possible restrictions on growth.
What changes its weight
Heavier in cross-border groups, after acquisitions and where one supplier supports several functions. Lighter where a small bank has a well-governed outsourced arrangement with clear decision rights.

Theme 2

Technical preparedness

Covers patching and malware protection, multi-factor authentication, vulnerability scanning, the incident response plan, and the disaster recovery plan and its testing.

Why it matters in banking
Trusted access is the main route in. Authentication protects the login; the attacks on record go around it through account resets and help desks. Recovery only counts if balances come back correct.
Where it went wrong
River Financial took systems offline after administrator accounts were compromised. The ECB's test of 109 banks against a lost core database found frameworks in place and recovery gaps behind them.
Road to credit
Broad outage, fraud, data exposure, reconciliation cost and liquidity pressure while records are rebuilt.
What changes its weight
Heavier if recovery systems share administrator access with production, or if the last recovery test restored servers but never validated transactions. Lighter with recent, scoped, business-validated tests.

Theme 3

Business continuity and cyber resilience

Covers the continuity plan, the cyber resilience plan, and how often they are tested and updated.

Why it matters in banking
Settlement, margin and payroll have hard deadlines. A fallback that keeps a minimum service can still fail to clear the queue in time, and that is when liquidity is called on.
Where it went wrong
ION's clients ran manually for weeks and met obligations only with clearing-house help. ICBC's US unit carried a temporary $9bn settlement obligation. Four Iranian banks stopped together through one shared network.
Road to credit
Missed settlement, emergency funding, lost fees, customer compensation.
What changes its weight
Heavier for digital-only delivery, deadline-sensitive businesses and a single outsourced core. Lighter where alternative channels and suppliers have been exercised at real volume with treasury in the room.

Theme 4

Insurance, certification and data protection

Covers cyber insurance, security certifications, data-protection compliance and the data protection officer.

Why it matters in banking
These are the answers most likely to be read as comfort. Insurance pays later and partially. A certificate covers the scope it names. Data harm can be severe with no interruption at all.
Where it went wrong
Standard Bank lost document-system data while core banking ran normally. Capital One's settlement arrived years after the breach. DriveWealth exposed records of clients who had already left.
Road to credit
Notification and protection costs, litigation, privacy penalties; immediate cash needs that insurance does not meet.
What changes its weight
Heavier with large retail data holdings, several reporting regimes and data retained at suppliers. Check policy sublimits, exclusions and waiting periods, and whether the certificate covers the critical services.

Theme 5

Learning from incidents

Covers post-incident analysis, regular risk assessments, and whether plans change as a result.

Why it matters in banking
Supervisors judge banks on whether findings are closed. Repeat findings are the clearest public signal of a governance problem, and they attract the remedies that cost most.
Where it went wrong
ECB supervisors found over 10% of critical-function contracts non-compliant and outstanding ICT findings still open. In the Korean cluster one bank's affected count rose from 119 to 2,210 as the investigation matured.
Road to credit
Recurring incidents, rising supervisory intensity, franchise damage and higher run-rate cost.
What changes its weight
Heavier with repeated overdue findings and no named owner. Lighter where fixes are retested: proof that the original failure no longer succeeds is stronger than proof that a change was made.

Where the questionnaire is silent, ask anyway: critical suppliers and their concentration, independent checks on high-value payments, and whether recovery is separate from the systems it is meant to recover. These three areas account for most of the consequential incidents in Plate 06.

Plate12

The conversation, and when to escalate

One question sits behind all the others: could this issue stop the borrower delivering essential services, reaching its liquidity or recovering trustworthy records, and could its finances absorb the result? The prompts below need no technical vocabulary on either side of the table.

Critical services

"Which services must continue even if your main technology is unavailable?"

A good answer names services, owners and a recovery order that has been tested with the business.

Privileged access

"How do you confirm that a request to reset an administrator's access is genuine?"

Listen for a process that does not rely on the help desk recognising a voice.

Recovery

"When did you last restore an essential service from recovery copies, and how did you confirm the balances were correct?"

A date, a scope and a business sign-off. Not a target restart time.

Independence

"Could a compromised employee or supplier account also reach your recovery systems?"

If production and recovery share one administrator path, redundancy is nominal.

Suppliers

"Which supplier failure would be hardest to work around, and for how long?"

Follow with whether recovery has been tested jointly, including loss of the supplier's own support.

Minimum service

"What could customers still do during a prolonged incident?"

And how operations, treasury and suppliers would coordinate if email and normal systems were down.

Payments

"What independent check protects a high-value payment or a change of beneficiary?"

Authentication alone is the Bangladesh Bank answer.

Data release

"Could sensitive customer information be released on the strength of an official-looking request alone?"

The Revolut case needed no intrusion.

Containment

"Could you isolate an affected area without stopping the whole bank?"

Ask which exercise last showed it.

Improvements a borrower can act on

  • Name an owner for each critical service and keep a one-page dependency map.
  • Test the whole identity chain, including help-desk resets and supplier access.
  • Prove a clean restore: data, keys and applications, with balances validated and backlog cleared.
  • Exercise the loss of a critical supplier and of that supplier's support.
  • Fix by business impact: unknown and unsupported assets behind critical services first.
  • Close findings by retesting, not by reporting completion.
