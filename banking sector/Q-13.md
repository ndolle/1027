# Banking: operational resilience and third-party concentration
**Evidence cutoff: 27 October 2026 | Worldwide scope, with EU, UK and US emphasis**

## 1. Preliminary sector assessment


**Structural disruption sensitivity: high.** Banking requires trustworthy records, timely processing and access to external payment and settlement infrastructure. An institution can remain solvent yet be unable to deliver essential services or complete obligations during an operational incident.

**Observed preparedness: established but uneven.** Documented workarounds have prevented defaults, while supervisory testing identifies recovery and supplier-management gaps. Neither outsourcing nor reliance on a major technology provider establishes inadequate resilience.

**Borrower-level exposure remains arrangement-specific.** Materiality depends on the affected service, shared dependencies, recoverable data, substitution capacity, disruption duration and accessible liquidity—not simply the number of suppliers or data centres.


## 2. Critical dependency matrix


This matrix describes characteristic dependencies, not verified architectures for every bank.

| Critical process | Technology, infrastructure and operating dependencies | Potential single point of failure | Substitutability and switching complexity |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Account servicing and transaction records** | Core platform, databases, identity, storage, reconciliation and specialist support | One ledger, privileged-access boundary or outsourced core supporting several channels | Replacement is generally a migration project, not an immediate fallback. Recovery must establish correct balances and transaction status |
| **Retail/commercial payments and cash access** | Payment engines, fraud/compliance checks, processors, card networks, telecoms, ATMs and cash logistics | Common processor or network connection; several channels sharing the same back end | Alternate rails may have different limits, cut-offs and finality. Cash requires functioning logistics and records |
| **Treasury, interbank settlement and liquidity management** | Settlement systems, messaging, correspondents, collateral records and authorised staff | Sole access intermediary, currency correspondent or settlement connection | Alternatives require pre-arranged accounts, connectivity, collateral and operating procedures |
| **Trading, clearing, margin and custody** | Market data, specialist software, clearinghouses, settlement agents, subcustodians and position records | Shared processing software or inability to calculate/reconcile obligations | Manual processing can preserve minimum activity but may not meet normal volume or deadlines |
| **Lending, origination and servicing** | Identity/credit data, documents, underwriting, collection/disbursement and funding interfaces | Servicer, verification provider or loan-sale interface | Existing servicing may continue while new lending stops. Migration requires reliable records and reconciled cash flows |
| **Digital channels and enterprise operations** | Cloud/hosting, DNS, identity, enterprise software, security tools, MSPs and remote support | Shared control plane, administrator, software update or underlying host | Another region/provider is useful only if the application and operations can actually run there |
| **Recovery and crisis management** | Clean copies, keys, identity, communications, staff, suppliers, power and facilities | Recovery requires the same compromised credentials or unavailable supplier | Independence must be demonstrated. Backup possession alone does not establish timely, safe recovery |

**Subsector emphasis:** digital-only banks depend heavily on remote channels; smaller outsourced-core users can face bundled-provider concentration; custody and markets businesses face deadline-sensitive settlement and collateral demands; sponsor-bank models require customer-level records and reconciliation across intermediaries.


## 3. Five documented disruption cases

### Case 1 — TSB migration failure: internal change and outsourced-delivery risk


**UK, April 2018; non-cyber ICT failure.** Retrospective reporting describes migration to a new platform followed by disruption across branch, telephone, online and mobile banking. A significant proportion of the bank’s **5.2 million customers** was affected. Regulatory findings concerned planning, testing, governance, operational-risk management and outsourcing oversight.

The reported **£48.65m combined FCA/PRA fines** and **£32.7m customer redress** are separate consequences. They are not a complete incident-cost estimate. **Confidence: moderate for this account**, because the retrieved sources are secondary retrospectives rather than the original enforcement notices. [1](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2281678645103689734) [2](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2281794961408000014)



**Duration qualification:** customer disruption and remediation continued over an extended period, but that must not be represented as every service being completely unavailable throughout. Exact restoration milestones require the original regulatory record.

**Lesson:** successful data migration is not sufficient evidence that the complete service will perform under live demand. Outsourced delivery does not remove the bank’s accountability.


### Case 2 — ION: shared processing interruption with effective workarounds


**Global derivatives operations, 30 January 2023; cyberattack.** FSOC records a LockBit attack affecting ION’s XTP services. **16 of approximately 60 CFTC-registered futures commission merchants** used XTP, with varying effects on processing, margin and reporting. Most services returned by **19 February**.

Manual processing and clearinghouse assistance supported margin obligations; **no defaults occurred**. Initial access, verified ransom and aggregate final loss are not established. **Confidence: high in the official account.** [3](https://home.treasury.gov/system/files/261/FSOC2023AnnualReport.pdf)



**Lesson:** supplier failure can interrupt several institutions without compromising their networks. Majority restoration after roughly three weeks is an incident milestone—not a sector recovery average or proof of continuous total outage.


### Case 3 — ICBC Financial Services: participant disruption and settlement exposure


**US securities subsidiary, November 2023; ransomware.** FS-ISAC’s March 2024 review describes affected systems being disconnected, disruption to US Treasury activity and equities-clearing problems. The initial-access route was unconfirmed. The incident should not be described as an outage across the entire ICBC group. **Confidence: high for disruption; limited for undisclosed technical details.** [4](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2279076498147121160)



Contemporaneous reporting describes a temporary **US$9bn obligation to BNY Mellon**. That is a settlement/funding exposure—not a US$9bn permanent incident loss. A claimed ransom payment was not independently verified in the retrieved account. Complete recovery duration and final net cost remain unknown. [5](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2278501787499495597)



**Lesson:** operational failure can create liquidity demands substantially different from—and potentially larger than—recognised remediation costs.


### Case 4 — CHAPS/Swift Y-Copy: common infrastructure dependency


**UK, 18 July 2024; non-cyber service failure.** Bank of England statistics record a **245-minute CHAPS settlement outage** associated with Swift’s Y-Copy service. CREST and retail net settlement were unaffected. Hours were extended and all transactions submitted to the Bank settled successfully.

No quantified direct loss is established here. **Confidence: high in the official operational record.** The 245-minute July event must not be conflated with a different certificate-related outage. [6](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/payment-and-settlement/payment-and-settlement-statistics)



**Lesson:** central or shared services can delay otherwise operational banks. Extending a processing window can preserve settlement, but depends on coordination and functioning participants.


### Case 5 — AWS outage: cross-sector cloud dependence


**20 October 2025; non-cyber cloud outage.** Contemporary coverage documents worldwide app and website disruption; European reporting identifies effects on several UK banks alongside other businesses. This supports correlated cloud-dependency exposure, but does not establish that every affected bank’s ledger or settlement capability failed.

Disruption lasted hours, with differing restoration experiences. A single validated banking-service recovery duration, bank-specific loss and complete provider/client recovery reconciliation were not established. **Confidence: moderate for banking impact; high for the broad outage.** [7](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2280695693150519306) [8](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2280698554336935938)



**Lesson:** provider restoration and customer-service restoration are separate milestones. A cloud event may affect customer interfaces, authentication or external integrations without disabling the core bank.


## 4. Third-party concentration indicators


Bitsight’s **November 2025** original study provides observable financial-sector indicators:

| Indicator | Finding | Interpretation limit |
|---|---|---|
| **Digital supplier relationships** | **41,511 financial organisations; 50,232 identified technology-provider relationships** | Broader than banking; observable connections are not a complete contractual or critical-service inventory |
| **Common technologies** | Global platforms alongside finance-specialist suppliers including Bloomberg, Murex, FactSet and Fiserv | Presence does not establish that a provider is indispensable to every customer |
| **Supplier security exposure** | Suppliers performed slightly worse than their customers across **16 of 22** measured risk vectors, with differences up to **15%** | External observations do not capture all compensating controls or internal resilience |
| **Supply-chain monitoring** | Nearly **600 financial-sector customers** monitored an average **36.3%** of inferred supply chains, versus **24.6%** for other organisations | Not a compliance score; some suppliers may be non-critical or monitored through other means |

The report also identifies specialist legacy-support and building-management dependencies. [9](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2280730163635307544)



**Regulatory recognition:** November 2025 reporting records EU designation of **19 critical technology providers**, including the European operations of major cloud providers. This recognises sector importance and introduces oversight; it does not establish their banking workload market shares or certify individual customer arrangements. [10](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2280763384905334785)



**Quantification gap:** no validated worldwide banking-specific market shares were established for critical cloud workloads, managed services, core processing or recovery services. Whole-cloud-market shares should not be relabelled as banking exposure.

Concentration should be measured by **essential functions and underlying dependencies**, not supplier count alone. A bank may have many vendors yet one identity platform, one settlement route or one specialist recovery team.


## 5. Substitutability and systemic exposure pathways



| Pathway | How one incident propagates | What determines resilience |
|---|---|---|
| **Common processor/software** | Several banks lose the same processing function | Independent records, fallback capacity, contractual cooperation and coordinated recovery |
| **Cloud, identity or managed administration** | Several applications or organisations share one control plane/access path | Independent administration and tested recovery—not merely geographic duplication |
| **Payment/settlement intermediary** | A participant cannot complete obligations to counterparties | Alternate access, collateral mobility, processing-window flexibility and liquidity |
| **Telecoms and energy** | Production, branches and recovery locations fail together | Physical route/site diversity, endurance and upstream supplier independence |
| **Shared software distribution** | A defective or malicious update reaches many organisations | Deployment controls, rollback and recovery unaffected by the same software |
| **Cash logistics and specialist support** | Banks cannot replenish cash or repair systems while providers face simultaneous demand | Pre-arranged capacity, retained skills, spares and realistic contingency priorities |

**Switching is often complex:** data portability is not application portability. Replacement can require integration, security approval, contractual changes, trained personnel, migrated records and reconciled transactions. Migration itself can introduce operational risk, as TSB illustrates.

Multi-cloud or multiple providers are not automatically superior: complexity may increase, while shared software, staff or identity leaves a common failure point intact.


## 6. Observed preparedness versus structural sensitivity


**Preparedness evidence:** the ECB’s July 2024 exercise tested response/recovery at **109 banks**, with enhanced testing at **28**. Frameworks were present, but recovery and dependency-related improvements were identified. The exercise assumed successful compromise; it was not an actual-incident count or a test establishing prevention effectiveness. [11](https://www.bankingsupervision.europa.eu/press/pr/date/2024/html/ssm.pr240726~06d5776a02.hu.html)



**Realised third-party involvement:** the FCA’s July 2026 publication states that **27% of firms’ reported 2025 incidents** involved third-party issues, of which **37% were cyber-related**. The population includes financial firms beyond banks. The figures do not measure the percentage of banks with inadequate controls. [12](https://www.fca.org.uk/news/blogs/strengthening-resilience-across-increasingly-interconnected-financial-system)



**Combined judgement:** ION and CHAPS show that coordination and alternatives can prevent more severe outcomes. TSB demonstrates that change governance and outsourced delivery can fail. Supervisory exercises show room for improvement, not uniform unreadiness.

The most useful maturity evidence is a recent test demonstrating:
- Minimum service under loss of a critical supplier.
- Clean restoration with correct records.
- Sustainable manual throughput and backlog clearance.
- Recovery access independent of compromised production.
- Liquidity and communications functioning throughout the scenario.


## 7. Financial consequences and credit interpretation


**Separate the accounting and cash-flow channels:**

- **Direct expense:** response, rebuilding, supplier support, overtime and customer assistance.
- **Redress and enforcement:** compensation and fines may arise later and must not be double-counted with provisions.
- **Revenue:** lost or deferred fees, transactions and originations; not all income stops during an outage.
- **Liquidity:** settlement obligations, collateral restrictions and replacement funding can matter before losses are finalised.
- **Franchise:** repeated service failures can weaken confidence and customer retention, but deposit flight must not be assumed without evidence.
- **Investment:** remediation or supplier migration can increase capital expenditure and recurring operating costs.

No defensible banking-wide average disruption loss or recovery duration is established by these five cases. Their endpoints differ: technical restoration, majority service recovery, reconciliation and completion of remediation are not interchangeable.


## 8. Conclusion, confidence and validation gaps


**Preliminary exposure assessment:** banking has **high inherent operational sensitivity and material common-dependency exposure**. Observed preparedness is mixed and institution-specific. Outsourcing can strengthen capability while concentrating dependence; internal provision can retain the same common points of failure.

**Confidence**
- **High:** shared-dependency mechanisms; official ION, CHAPS and ECB findings.
- **Moderate:** vendor-observed concentration indicators, contemporary AWS banking effects and secondary TSB details.
- **Limited:** undisclosed supplier architectures, exact switching times, complete net losses and emerging-market comparability.

**Priority validation gaps:** original TSB enforcement/restoration chronology; bank-level AWS impacts; critical-workload provider concentration; underlying fourth parties; tested substitution/recovery throughput; and borrower-specific liquidity and loss absorption.

For credit discussions, the decisive question is: **Can this borrower complete essential transactions with trustworthy records when its hardest-to-replace dependency is unavailable—and fund itself until normal service is restored?**
