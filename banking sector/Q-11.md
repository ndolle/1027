# Banking: fraud risk exposure assessment
**Evidence cutoff: 27 October 2026 | Scope: worldwide, with EU, UK and US emphasis**

## 1. Preliminary inherent exposure assessment


**Banking has high inherent fraud exposure** because it holds transferable assets, executes payments, extends credit against representations and collateral, and delegates authority to employees, customers and intermediaries. Exposure spans three distinct roles:

- **Victim:** the bank loses its own money or assets.
- **Payment intermediary:** customer fraud proceeds move through its accounts or infrastructure.
- **Responsible institution:** reimbursement, conduct, monitoring or governance obligations create liability.

This is a qualitative sector assessment—not a finding that banks generally have ineffective controls. **Residual risk cannot be assessed without borrower-specific evidence of prevention, detection, recovery and financial capacity.**


## 2. Five principal fraud scenarios



| Scenario | Mechanism and cyber/non-cyber distinction | Most exposed activities | Potential consequences |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Payment diversion and authorised-payment deception** | BEC, supplier/CEO impersonation, altered invoice or beneficiary details, investment and bank-impersonation scams. Email compromise is cyber-enabled; a deceptive telephone call or forged letter need not involve an intrusion | Corporate payments, private banking, conveyancing, treasury and customer transfers | Customer loss, reimbursement, recovery costs, payment disputes and reputational harm |
| **2. Identity fraud and unauthorised account/card use** | Stolen or synthetic identity, account takeover, credential/OTP theft, fraudulent card applications, cheque alteration and cash-out | Digital onboarding, consumer credit, cards, remote banking and ATM access | Direct losses, chargebacks, collections against fictitious borrowers, remediation and restrictions on accounts |
| **3. Insider theft, procurement corruption and asset misappropriation** | Employees or contractors divert cash, alter records, create fictitious vendors, approve inflated invoices, accept kickbacks or collude with outsiders | Branch/cash operations, accounts payable, lending, vendor management and privileged administration | Repeated undetected losses, unreliable records, investigation costs and management disruption |
| **4. Lending, collateral and trade-finance fraud** | False borrower accounts, fictitious receivables, duplicate financing, forged guarantees/letters of credit, diverted collections and intermediary collusion | Commercial lending, asset-based lending, mortgage servicing, trade finance and supply-chain finance | Credit impairment, unenforceable collateral, concentrated exposures, funding demands and litigation |
| **5. Management, financial-reporting and concealed-position fraud** | False sales metrics, unauthorised products, manipulated valuations or provisions, hidden trading positions and management override | Universal/investment banks, sales-led retail models and complex groups | Misstated earnings/capital, unexpected losses, customer redress, regulatory restrictions and loss of confidence |

**Overlap matters:** supplier impersonation can affect the bank’s own procurement or a corporate customer’s payments. A fraudulent borrower’s financial statements create bank credit exposure even where the bank’s own reporting is accurate.


## 3. Five documented cases and their causes



| Case | Established facts and financial consequences | Cause/control relevance and limitations |
|---|---|---|
| **Wells Fargo unauthorised accounts—US; misconduct during 2002–16** | Carnegie Mellon’s Software Engineering Institute describes excessive sales targets, unauthorised accounts/products, forged signatures and transfers. Its September 2024 account records approximately **US$3bn in fines/legal settlements** | **Internal conduct fraud**, not an external cyberattack. Evidence connects incentives and organisational culture to misconduct. US$3bn is not money stolen from customers or necessarily the complete lifetime cost. **Confidence: moderate–high; authoritative retrospective, not the original settlement instrument.** [1](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2279505582559856340) |
| **Wells Fargo employee embezzlement—US; sentencing 1 June 2026** | DOJ records a guilty plea and an **18-month sentence** for Tamim Haidar. Its account describes theft exceeding **US$800,000**, involving ATM cash and false database entries | **Insider asset misappropriation with electronic concealment**, not an external intrusion. Record falsification is established; the evidence does not establish exactly which reconciliation or supervisory control failed. Restitution is not proof of completed recovery. **Confidence: high.** [2](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2281498447385200998) |
| **Forged bank-document scheme involving SBI verification—India; convictions reported 2 May 2026** | Court reporting records **seven convictions** in an **₹8.64 crore** fraud involving forged documents and shell entities | Primarily **document/impersonation fraud**. Verification was subverted through access to a bank-branch telephone and delivery of verification documents to unauthorised recipients. Final recoveries and net bank loss are not established. **Confidence: moderate–high; court reporting rather than original judgment.** [3](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2281429836423495680) |
| **U.S. Mortgage servicing scheme against Wells Fargo—US; guilty plea 12 June 2013** | DOJ records the former servicing company CEO’s guilty plea in a scheme to defraud Wells Fargo of **more than US$8m** | **Intermediary/servicing fraud**, involving withheld funds rather than a documented external hack. Demonstrates exposure to collections handled outside the lender. Detailed control failure, final net loss and recovery are not established in the retrieved record. **Confidence: high for the plea and scheme amount.** [4](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2266886037240283136) |




**Bangladesh Bank—Bangladesh/cross-border, February 2016.** DOJ documents fraudulent payment instructions through compromised bank-managed infrastructure: approximately **US$81m** reached Philippine accounts, while a separate **US$20m transfer was stopped**. This was **cyber-enabled payment fraud**, not compromise of SWIFT’s central infrastructure. US attribution to a North Korean conspiracy is an official charging allegation; the complaint is not a conviction. Current net loss after recoveries is not established here. **Confidence: high in the documented case assertions, with legal-status qualification.** [5](https://www.justice.gov/d9/press-releases/attachments/2018/09/06/2018_09_06_park_complaint_unsealed_0.pdf)



These cases illustrate distinct mechanisms, not the most statistically representative frauds. No sufficiently substantiated EU financial-statement case is substituted merely to achieve geographic balance.


## 4. Quantitative evidence and five-year trends

### UK: primary, comparable administrative series


UK Finance’s **27 May 2025 report** supplies a consistent five-year table. Values are **gross losses in nominal GBP**, including money subsequently recovered. Cases count affected cards/accounts—not unique people or banks.

| Calendar year | Total gross payment-fraud losses | Confirmed cases | APP gross losses |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| **2020** | £1,204.6m | 3,065,123 | £420.7m |
| **2021** | £1,313.6m | 3,108,463 | £583.2m |
| **2022** | £1,212.1m | 2,988,683 | £485.2m |
| **2023** | £1,168.4m | 2,967,361 | £459.7m |
| **2024** | £1,172.6m | 3,313,684 | £450.7m |

**Method:** member-reported data from banks, card issuers/acquirers and other financial providers, using agreed definitions and plausibility checks. This is not a random sample or a census of all fraud. Data can be restated; APP reporting includes closed cases, creating timing effects. Totals are not banks’ net accounting losses. [6](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2280358528705118649)



**Calculated trend:** 2020–24 gross losses declined approximately **2.7%**, while recorded cases increased approximately **8.1%**. Different changes in volume and value show why case counts alone cannot measure financial severity or control effectiveness.



**2024 typology detail:**

- Invoice/mandate fraud: **£42.7m across 2,301 cases**; **£32.3m** involved non-personal accounts.
- CEO impersonation: **£11.8m across 270 cases**, with an average exceeding **£40,000**.
- Card fraud: **£572.6m**; remote-purchase losses rose **11%**, while remote-banking losses fell **7%**.
- Industry reported preventing **£1.45bn** of unauthorised fraud—approximately **67p per £1 attempted** in the measured population.
- **£267.1m** was returned to APP victims through refunds or recovered funds; this is not solely bank-funded reimbursement. [6](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2280358528705118649)



Dividing published rounded values by counts gives approximate **2024 gross means** of **£354 per recorded case overall**, **£2,427 per APP case** and **£18,557 per invoice/mandate case**. These are arithmetic ratios—not medians, losses per customer or expected bank losses.


### Latest indications and cross-jurisdiction limits


**UK 2025—secondary evidence:** June 2026 coverage reports **£1.28bn** and **3.81m cases**, including **£576.4m APP losses**. However, its reported year-on-year movements do not reconcile with the original 2024 table above. Possible restatement or reporting changes require checking against the original 2026 report. These figures should not be appended as a clean continuation without reconciliation. [7](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2281531366682132497)



**EU—indicative, not fully validated:** August 2026 secondary coverage attributes **€4.2bn for 2024** and **€3.5bn for 2023** to EBA/ECB reporting. The retrieved evidence does not adequately establish the precise reporting population or distinguish loss allocation from fraudulent transaction value. These are not used as a bank-loss benchmark. [8](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2281665716178386950)



**US and worldwide gaps:** complaint datasets and occupational-fraud studies are not equivalent to banking-sector loss accounts. No defensible worldwide banking-specific median, mean annual loss per bank or five-year US/EU comparable series was validated. ACFE’s all-industry loss estimates must not be applied to bank revenue. Customer, merchant and bank losses must not be summed across overlapping datasets.


## 5. Structural exposure and control weaknesses



| Exposure driver | Why banking is exposed | Controls to examine—not presumed failures |
|---|---|---|
| **Fast, cross-border payments** | Funds can be dispersed before recall; several institutions share incomplete information | Beneficiary verification, independent callbacks, transaction monitoring and rapid interbank escalation |
| **High-volume cards and remote banking** | Small repeated losses can coexist with larger targeted transfers | Authentication lifecycle, device/session controls, wallet enrolment, anomaly detection and disputes handling |
| **Lending against documents and representations** | False revenue, assets, ownership or receivables can defeat ordinary credit analysis | Independent verification, collateral/receivable checks, connected-party analysis and duplicate-financing detection |
| **Intermediaries and outsourced servicing** | Bank may rely on another party’s records, collections or customer verification | Reconciliation, direct access to records, collections oversight and exception escalation |
| **Employees controlling cash, payments or records** | Access can support both theft and concealment | Separation of approval, custody and reconciliation; independent review; protected reporting channels |
| **Sales and reporting incentives** | Targets can reward misstatement or concealment | Balanced remuneration, challenge of exceptional performance, complaint analysis and effective escalation |
| **Procurement/supplier changes** | Legitimate invoices and trusted vendor names can conceal diversion or kickbacks | Independent vendor-master changes, conflict declarations, invoice matching and payment approval |

Financial-statement manipulation can occur at the bank or its borrower. Credit assessment should separately test the reliability of the bank’s own capital/earnings and the representations supporting its loan book.



**Observed effectiveness is mixed, not absent.** UK Finance reports declining counterfeit-card and remote-banking fraud alongside growing remote-purchase fraud. It identifies social engineering of one-time passcodes used for transactions or digital-wallet registration. Its prevention figures demonstrate recorded interventions, but do not prove that one technology caused the overall trend. [6](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2280358528705118649)


## 6. Business, financial and regulatory consequences


**Financial transmission:** distinguish gross stolen funds, recoveries, customer reimbursement, legal penalties and investigation expenditure. Fraud can also cause credit write-offs, temporary liquidity demands, trapped funds, lost customers and increased compliance costs. Prolonged concealment can undermine reported earnings and capital. These consequences are plausible pathways—not automatic outcomes or quantified losses for an individual bank.



**UK binding reimbursement:** mandatory APP arrangements began **7 October 2024**, covering qualifying domestic Faster Payments and CHAPS payments, with an **£85,000** limit and eligibility conditions. Protected populations include personal customers, microbusinesses and relevant charities—not all corporate payments. UK Finance’s wider statistics include payments outside that mandatory perimeter. [6](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2280358528705118649)



**EU payee verification:** September 2026 legal reporting describes the European Payments Council scheme matching the intended payee’s name/identifier against account information before a SEPA transfer. The customer can still proceed after the result, so verification does not eliminate deception risk. Scheme rules must be distinguished from the applicable legislation and entity-specific implementation deadlines. [9](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2281771697935745025)



**Additional applicability checks:**

- **EU:** payment-security/authentication, unauthorised-payment liability, governance and AML obligations; DORA addresses enabling ICT risk but is not a complete fraud regime.
- **UK:** financial-crime systems and controls, customer treatment and the large-organisation failure-to-prevent-fraud regime. Fraud committed for an organisation’s benefit differs from fraud against it; statutory scope and reasonable-prevention provisions require legal review.
- **US:** consumer electronic-transfer protections differ from commercial payment rules and from payments genuinely authorised under deception. Bank security, suspicious-activity reporting and state-law duties can coexist; no universal reimbursement rule should be assumed.
- **Across jurisdictions:** AML controls address criminal proceeds and related risks, but an AML failure is not itself proof of fraud, and suspicious-activity reports are not convictions.

Detailed current legal texts and thresholds were not fully validated in this investigation; these are applicability priorities, not a compliance opinion.


## 7. Assessment and evidence confidence


**Preliminary inherent exposure: high; residual exposure: undetermined.** Payment and identity fraud create recurrent volume exposure; insider, credit and reporting fraud can create concentrated tail losses that payment statistics do not capture.

Materiality rises with weak independent verification, concentrated authority, opaque intermediaries, unreliable reconciliation, aggressive incentives and limited loss absorption. It may be reduced by demonstrated control performance, rapid recovery and strong financial buffers—but not by policy statements alone.

**Confidence and method:**
- **High:** the original UK Finance historical table and cited DOJ case facts.
- **Moderate:** secondary court reporting and the Wells Fargo retrospective.
- **Limited:** unreconciled 2025 UK updates, EU aggregate definitions and worldwide banking median/average losses.
- Cases were selected for mechanism and documentation, not statistical representativeness. No case count has been converted into a probability.

**Priority validation gaps:** obtain original 2026 UK Finance and EBA/ECB reports; validate banking-specific occupational-fraud denominators; reconcile gross versus net losses; and obtain borrower-specific fraud losses, recoveries, reimbursement, detection times and repeated control findings.
