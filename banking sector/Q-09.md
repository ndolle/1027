# Banking: provisional questionnaire mapping and Credit Officer discussion guide
**Research basis: findings established in this chat through 27 October 2026**  
**Audience: Senior Credit Officers | Purpose: constructive client engagement, not a cybersecurity audit**

## 1. Engagement principles and priorities


The central credit question is: **Could this issue prevent the borrower from delivering essential services, accessing liquidity or recovering trustworthy records—and could its financial resources absorb the consequences?**

Prioritise discussion in this order:

1. **Transaction integrity and recoverability:** balances, payments, positions and customer records.
2. **Common points of failure:** privileged identities, critical suppliers and recovery infrastructure.
3. **Minimum service and liquidity:** practical alternatives, settlement access and funding capacity.
4. **Containment and customer-data protection:** limiting the duration and scope of harm.

For each answer, distinguish **“not in place,” “outsourced,” “not applicable,” “not known” and “not independently tested.”** These responses have different implications.


## 2. Incident evidence supporting the mapping

### Identity and privileged access


River Financial’s June 2026 disclosure records ransomware, affected administrative accounts and systems taken offline. It does **not** establish that MFA was absent, how initial access occurred or that backups were destroyed. It supports discussion of privileged access and containment without diagnosing an undisclosed control failure. [1](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1641601/000119312526282946/ck0001641601-20260619.htm)


### Configuration and risk governance


Capital One’s 2019 breach involved a misconfigured web application firewall. The OCC’s 2020 action identified deficiencies in cloud-migration risk assessment and delayed correction, imposing an **US$80m penalty**. This establishes a configuration/governance relationship—not that cloud use itself indicates weak resilience. [2](https://www.justice.gov/usao-wdwa/united-states-v-paige-thompson) [3](https://www.occ.gov/news-issuances/news-releases/2020/nr-occ-2020-101.html)


### Supplier continuity and operational workarounds


The January 2023 ION ransomware incident interrupted derivatives processing, margin calculations and reporting. Most services returned by 19 February; manual processing and clearinghouse support enabled obligations to be met without defaults. Initial access was not established in FSOC’s account. [4](https://home.treasury.gov/system/files/261/FSOC2023AnnualReport.pdf)


### Payment integrity


The Bangladesh Bank case demonstrates fraudulent instructions generated through a compromised bank-managed payment environment. It does not establish compromise of SWIFT’s central network. The case supports discussion of independent authorisation, monitoring and payment verification. [5](https://www.justice.gov/d9/press-releases/attachments/2018/09/06/2018_09_06_park_complaint_unsealed_0.pdf)


### Data exposure without core-service interruption


Standard Bank’s April 2026 update identified affected administrative/document systems while reporting that core and transactional systems remained available. This demonstrates why confidentiality consequences must be considered separately from business interruption. It does not establish the initial-access method. [6](https://www.standardbank.co.za/southafrica/news-and-media/newsroom/an-important-data-incident-update-14-april)


### Recovery preparedness


The ECB’s 2024 exercise assessed response and recovery at 109 banks against a fictitious successful attack affecting core databases. It identified improvement needs. This is supervisory exercise evidence, not a historical attack or proof that all participating banks lacked the same control. [7](https://www.bankingsupervision.europa.eu/press/pr/date/2024/html/ssm.pr240726~06d5776a02.hu.html)


## 3. Questionnaire-to-threat-to-incident-to-credit mapping


**Provisional topics below are not questionnaire IDs.** The incident references point to the evidence above. “Weakness” means a potential issue to clarify, not an established finding.

| Questionnaire topic / priority | Potential issue indicated by an adverse answer | Threat pathway and incident relevance | Operational dependencies and plausible credit consequences |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Critical services, accountability and dependency mapping — highest priority** | No agreed view of essential services, owners, disruption tolerance or dependencies | An isolated incident becomes prolonged because recovery priorities are incomplete. **ION** demonstrates the importance of processing dependencies; **ECB testing** supports scrutiny of recovery planning | Core ledger, payment routes, treasury, staff and suppliers. Possible missed settlement, customer harm, fee loss, emergency funding and remediation expense |
| **Identity, MFA, account recovery and privileged access — highest priority** | Weak protection of administrators, supplier identities or account resets; excessive standing access | Credential theft or recovery abuse → privileged access → data theft/ransomware. **River** illustrates administrative-account involvement; it does not prove absent MFA | Identity services, cloud administration, remote support and backup access. Possible broad outage, fraud, data exposure and impaired recovery |
| **Asset visibility, vulnerability and configuration management — high priority** | Unknown exposed systems, unsupported components or overdue high-impact remediation | Exposed application or configuration error → access to sensitive systems/data. **Capital One** is confirmed configuration/governance evidence; it is not proof of an unpatched-CVE pathway | Internet banking, APIs, remote services and cloud permissions. Possible breach costs, service isolation, legal liability and accelerated replacement spending |
| **Segmentation, containment and monitoring — high priority** | A local compromise may spread; critical identity/cloud/payment activity is not visible | Valid-account abuse or malware → lateral movement/exfiltration → wider containment shutdown. **River** demonstrates containment actions; public evidence does not establish its segmentation or detection weakness | Management infrastructure, endpoints, databases and supplier connections. Possible longer interruption, wider data scope and uncertain restoration decisions |
| **Backups, data integrity and recovery testing — highest priority** | Copies exist but restoration, administrative independence or transaction correctness is untested | Encryption or corruption → inability to restore trustworthy records. **ECB testing** provides direct preparedness evidence; ransomware alone does not prove backup failure | Ledger journals, keys, configurations, identity and recovery sites. Possible extended outage, reconciliation cost, compensation and liquidity pressure |
| **Third-party access, concentration and exit — highest priority** | Reliance on assurance documents without service-specific testing; hidden shared providers or impractical alternatives | Supplier compromise → processing outage or downstream data exposure. **ION** demonstrates interrupted processing, not compromise of every customer’s network | Outsourced core, clearing software, cloud, telecoms and managed services. Possible correlated interruption, replacement expense and limited recovery leverage |
| **Incident response, continuity and crisis communications — highest priority** | Unclear authority, unavailable contacts or fallbacks that cannot sustain minimum service | Delayed containment or uncoordinated shutdown → backlogs and customer harm. **ION** shows the value of coordinated workarounds; **River** shows active containment | Operations, treasury, customer support, legal and suppliers. Possible settlement delays, funding needs, increased expense and inconsistent reporting |
| **Payment, ledger and customer-data safeguards — highest priority where applicable** | Authentication is treated as sufficient authorisation; reconciliation or sensitive-data controls are weak | Trusted access → fraudulent instruction or data release. **Bangladesh Bank** demonstrates payment manipulation; **Standard Bank** separates data harm from core outage | Payment approvals, beneficiaries, records, document stores and client servicing. Possible theft, refunds, litigation, lost relationships and capital erosion |
| **Facilities, ATMs and upstream infrastructure — context-dependent** | Nominal redundancy shares power, carrier routes or maintenance access; physical/facilities dependencies are unclear | Upstream outage or physical/software compromise → loss of site or customer access. The cited cases do not establish a bank facilities-OT failure | Power, cooling, telecoms, cash logistics and ATM support. Possible regional outage, cash-access restrictions and slower recovery |



### Regulatory interpretation of these topics


- **EU:** assess relevant DORA obligations for ICT risk, continuity/testing, incidents and suppliers, alongside GDPR and payment requirements.
- **UK:** assess applicable important-business-service/impact-tolerance rules, outsourcing expectations and existing notification duties. Do not treat future reporting arrangements as already operative.
- **US:** establish charter-based security and notification requirements, applicable state rules, and any securities or nonbank obligations.
- **Across jurisdictions:** determine legal scope before drawing conclusions. A negative questionnaire answer is not, by itself, a finding of non-compliance. A particular product, architecture or certification is not universally mandatory.


## 4. Materiality conditions: what changes the credit assessment?



| Dimension | May increase materiality | May reduce—but not eliminate—materiality |
|---|---|---|
| **Business model** | Deadline-sensitive settlement; digital-only delivery; concentrated origination income; reliance on one outsourced core | Tested alternative channels and diversified earnings that remain operational |
| **Geography** | Conflict exposure; limited infrastructure diversity; multiple reporting regimes; restrictions on support or data movement | Independent recovery locations and confirmed legal/operational access |
| **Compensating controls** | None demonstrated, or all depend on the same identity/provider | Tested alternative authorisation, restricted access, isolation and manual processing |
| **Concentration** | One provider supports core processing, channels and recovery; shared underlying subcontractor | Demonstrated independence and executable substitution—not merely separate contracts |
| **Financial resilience** | Thin liquidity/capital headroom; deposit concentration; funding maturities; limited loss absorption | Accessible liquidity, sustainable earnings and credible support |
| **Evidence quality** | Repeated overdue findings; no owner; inconsistent answers; no test results | Recent, scoped testing with business validation and independently retested remediation |

A smaller institution can be resilient using well-governed outsourcing. A large institution can remain concentrated despite multiple data centres. Insurance may offset some eventual costs but should not be assumed available to meet immediate settlement or liquidity needs.


## 5. Non-technical discussion prompts and practical improvements


### Critical services and governance
**Ask:**
- “Which services must continue even if your main technology is unavailable?”
- “Who decides the recovery order, and when was that order last tested with the business?”

**Improve:** assign service owners; maintain concise dependency maps; link tolerances and remediation to customer and financial consequences.

### Identity and privileged access
**Ask:**
- “How do you confirm that a request to reset an administrator’s access is genuine?”
- “Could a compromised employee or supplier account also reach your recovery systems?”

**Improve:** strengthen high-risk authentication and recovery; restrict privileges; review supplier and emergency access.

### Assets, vulnerabilities and configurations
**Ask:**
- “How do you know all systems supporting essential services are included in maintenance?”
- “When an urgent weakness cannot be fixed immediately, who accepts the risk and what protection is used?”

**Improve:** reconcile inventories; prioritise exposed critical services; verify fixes and time-limit exceptions.

### Containment and monitoring
**Ask:**
- “Could you isolate an affected area without stopping the whole bank?”
- “What recent exercise showed that suspicious access would be recognised and escalated?”

**Improve:** test isolation decisions; extend visibility to identities, cloud administration and critical supplier interfaces.

### Backups and trustworthy recovery
**Ask:**
- “When did you last restore an essential service using recovery copies rather than the live system?”
- “How did you confirm that balances and unfinished transactions were correct?”

**Improve:** protect recovery administration; restore full service chains; validate records and backlog clearance.

### Third parties and concentration
**Ask:**
- “Which supplier failure would be hardest to work around, and for how long?”
- “Have you tested recovery with that supplier, including loss of its support?”

**Improve:** focus assurance on critical services; identify underlying dependencies; test joint recovery and realistic exit options.

### Incident response and continuity
**Ask:**
- “What minimum service could customers use during a prolonged incident?”
- “If ordinary email and system access failed, how would operations, treasury and suppliers coordinate?”

**Improve:** clarify authority; maintain independent communications; exercise minimum service, liquidity and customer-support arrangements together.

### Payment and data safeguards
**Ask:**
- “What independent check protects a high-value payment or beneficiary change?”
- “Could sensitive customer information be released on the strength of an apparently official request alone?”

**Improve:** independently verify high-consequence instructions; reconcile transactions; restrict and monitor sensitive-data release.

### Facilities and upstream dependencies
**Ask:**
- “Do your backup locations or connections share underlying infrastructure with the main site?”
- “How long can essential sites and cash services operate without normal power, connectivity or maintenance?”

**Improve:** validate actual independence and endurance; control remote maintenance; test relevant physical and facilities contingencies.


## 6. Escalation indicators and proportional response



| Indicator | Appropriate response—not an automatic credit action |
|---|---|
| No demonstrated restoration of the core ledger or essential payment service | Request a service-level recovery summary; consider specialist review |
| Recovery depends on the same potentially compromised identity or administrator access | Clarify independence and compensating controls with specialists |
| Critical supplier has no tested workaround, joint recovery or usable exit | Review concentration, contractual access and minimum-service options |
| Repeated overdue findings affecting essential operations | Seek ownership, milestones, retest evidence and closer monitoring |
| Uncertain balances, positions, reconciliation or access to customer funds | Prompt operational/treasury and specialist engagement |
| Confirmed incident with unresolved essential-service or liquidity effects | Obtain factual updates on service capacity, cash needs and recovery milestones |
| Thin financial headroom combined with prolonged interruption | Run borrower-specific liquidity, earnings and capital sensitivities |
| Broad assurances unsupported by scope, date or testing | Request proportionate evidence before relying on the assurance |
| Possible missed reporting obligation or unclear legal applicability | Refer to legal/compliance expertise; do not diagnose a breach from the questionnaire |

Escalation can mean seeking clarification, obtaining specialist input or increasing monitoring. It does not necessarily mean repricing, reducing limits or declining credit.


## 7. Suggested Credit Officer record


For each material topic, record:

- **Client statement:** what was said, by whom and when.
- **Evidence status:** self-report only; supporting document; test result; or specialist-confirmed finding.
- **Operational relevance:** affected service, dependency and plausible interruption.
- **Mitigants:** alternatives, compensating controls and financial buffers.
- **Uncertainty:** facts not yet established.
- **Agreed action:** owner, proportionate milestone and review date.
- **Credit interpretation:** potential consequence and sensitivity—not an unsupported loss estimate or probability.

**Overall conclusion:** give greatest weight to demonstrated ability to preserve trustworthy records, complete essential transactions and access liquidity during disruption. Use questionnaire answers to identify where that evidence is needed, while recognising proportionate controls and credible alternatives.
