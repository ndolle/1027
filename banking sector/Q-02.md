# Banking: critical operational processes and dependencies
**Research date: 27 October 2026 | Geographic scope: worldwide, with EU, UK and US distinctions**

## Executive findings


**Shared service providers can create operational concentration across otherwise separate banks.** The Financial Stability Board identifies third-party dependencies as a potential financial-stability concern; US interagency guidance specifically highlights dependence on one provider for multiple activities and technology interoperability constraints. These are documented risk channels, not evidence that every bank has an unmitigated single point of failure. ([fsb.org](https://www.fsb.org/2023/12/fsb-publishes-toolkit-for-enhancing-third-party-risk-management-and-oversight/?trk=article-ssr-frontend-pulse_little-text-block&utm_source=openai))



**Assessment — high confidence in the dependency categories, moderate confidence in institution-level criticality:** the most consequential interruption points are the transaction system of record, identity and privileged access, payment and settlement connectivity, critical outsourced processing, and the infrastructure needed to recover them. Geographic redundancy alone does not establish independence if production and recovery share credentials, software, management infrastructure or suppliers.

A dependency becomes a **single point of failure** when its loss cannot be bypassed within the affected service’s tolerable interruption period. The maps below distinguish documented sector dependencies from analytical scenarios; they do not assign undisclosed vendor relationships to individual banks.


## 1. Subsector dependency map


**Analytical map:** these are characteristic service chains, not verified architectures for every institution. “Bottleneck” identifies where service delivery could stop despite other systems remaining available.

| Subsector / business model | Critical service-delivery chain | Principal bottlenecks and continuity sensitivities |
|---|---|---|
| **Retail and commercial banks** | Customer authentication → account/loan records → fraud and compliance checks → payment processing → clearing/settlement → reconciliation | Core ledger availability and integrity; payment authorisation; screening; branch, ATM and digital-channel access. Multiple customer channels may still share one back end. |
| **Community and regional banks; smaller mutuals/cooperatives** | Often outsourced core processing → bundled digital banking and payments → external networks | A core provider can support several critical functions simultaneously. Limited in-house capability can constrain migration and emergency operation. |
| **Universal banks** | Retail/commercial chains plus trading, custody, treasury and cross-border services | Shared group technology, identity, data and operations can connect otherwise diversified businesses. Country-level recovery can depend on group infrastructure. |
| **Investment banks, broker-dealers and prime brokers** | Market data → order/risk systems → execution → clearing → margin/collateral → settlement and reporting | Execution connectivity, accurate positions, clearing access and intraday collateral mobilisation. Manual processing can become a capacity bottleneck. |
| **Custody and transaction banks** | Client instructions → securities/cash records → settlement agents and infrastructures → reconciliation → corporate actions and reporting | Settlement connectivity, asset-position integrity, subcustodians, cash correspondents and deadline-sensitive corporate actions. |
| **Licensed digital banks / neobanks** | App/API → identity → core/cloud services → payment processors and networks → customer support | App availability is insufficient if ledger, identity or payment services fail. Digital-only delivery reduces branch-based workarounds. |
| **Banking-as-a-service and sponsor-bank models** | Fintech interface → middleware/program manager → bank records and controls → payment rails | Fragmented responsibility, customer-level records, reconciliation and access to funds. Several fintech brands may depend on one sponsor or middleware provider. |
| **Specialist mortgage, consumer and SME lenders** | Origination → identity/credit data → underwriting → disbursement → servicing and collections → funding | Credit-bureau and verification interfaces, loan-servicing records, payment collection and funding interfaces. Origination may stop before existing-loan servicing does. |



### Evidence supporting the business-model distinctions


- **US core-provider dependency — historical structural evidence, high confidence:** the Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City’s **27 March 2024** study identifies Fiserv, FIS and Jack Henry as the dominant US core-provider group. It documents their roles in account processing and connections supporting ACH, wire, check, card and ATM transactions. This establishes a concentration channel, not a current 2026 market-share estimate. ([kansascityfed.org](https://www.kansascityfed.org/research/payments-system-research-briefings/market-structure-of-core-banking-services-providers/?utm_source=openai))
- **US sponsor-bank/BaaS dependency — official evidence, high confidence:** the agencies’ **25 July 2024** joint statement documents arrangements in which third parties maintain deposit and transaction records, process payments, perform compliance functions and provide customer-facing technology and servicing. The statement does not imply that every arrangement outsources all these functions. ([federalreserve.gov](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/files/bcreg20240725c1.pdf?utm_source=openai))
- **Investment-banking dependency — observed historical cyber incident, high confidence:** the CFTC’s **2 February 2023** statement confirms that the ION incident affected a provider of cleared-derivatives order management, execution, trading and trade processing, delaying some clearing members’ data submissions and the Commitments of Traders report. ([cftc.gov](https://www.cftc.gov/PressRoom/SpeechesTestimony/cftcstatement020223?utm_source=openai))


## 2. Critical systems, disruption mechanisms and alternatives


**Reading the matrix:** cyber mechanisms and consequences below are **plausible scenarios**, not assertions of observed exploitation. Alternatives are continuity options whose effectiveness requires institution-specific testing. **Critical** means potentially necessary for transaction completion or safe restoration; **high** means capable of materially interrupting a business line.

| Critical dependency and business function | Plausible cyber disruption → operational consequence | Alternatives, constraints and continuity importance |
|---|---|---|
| **Core banking, loan servicing, databases, transaction ledger and reconciliation** — authoritative balances, obligations and transaction status | Ransomware, destructive activity or unauthorised record changes → unavailable accounts, unreliable balances, blocked disbursements and reconciliation backlogs | Clean backups, isolated recovery and reconciled journals. Restarting applications does not establish data correctness. Replacing a core platform is not an immediate outage workaround. **Critical.** |
| **Identity, directories, privileged-access management, certificates, HSMs and key management** — authenticate users/services and authorise sensitive actions | Identity compromise, certificate failure or loss of signing/decryption capability → legitimate users and applications cannot transact, or systems must be isolated | Independently accessible emergency credentials, resilient key infrastructure and tested certificate recovery. Recovery tools must not require the same failed identity path. **Critical.** |
| **Payment engines, gateways, card processors and fraud/sanctions screening** — validate, authorise and route transfers | Processor compromise, DDoS, corrupted instructions or unavailable screening → rejected/queued payments, missed cut-offs and manual exceptions | Alternate channels or processors only where contracted, integrated and tested. Another payment rail may have different limits, timing and finality. **Critical.** |
| **Messaging, RTGS, clearing, CCPs, securities settlement and correspondent banks** — interbank communication, final settlement, clearing and access to currencies/markets | Cyber incident at a participant, intermediary or shared infrastructure → settlement delays, trapped liquidity, incomplete positions and delayed collateral movements | Pre-arranged alternate access, correspondents and infrastructure contingency procedures. Final settlement infrastructures cannot generally be replaced on demand. **Critical.** |
| **Trading, market data, pricing, risk and collateral systems** — execute trades, value positions and calculate exposure/margin | Compromised feeds, unavailable trading software or inconsistent positions → trading restrictions, uncertain valuations and delayed margin/reporting | Secondary data sources and controlled manual processing; capacity, contractual access and reconciliation constrain substitution. **Critical for markets businesses.** |
| **Cloud, hosting, storage, virtualisation and data-centre control planes** — run applications and hold operational data | Tenant or administrator compromise, destructive changes or shared-service outage → simultaneous failure of applications and recovery capability | Independent recovery environments and portable data; cross-region deployment may retain common software or identity dependencies. Multi-cloud does not automatically make an application portable. **Critical where hosting essential services.** |
| **Managed service providers, outsourced operations and remote support** — administer infrastructure, process transactions and support recovery | Compromised remote-management tools or provider credentials → access to several clients; supplier isolation can itself stop services | Segmented administration, independent access, retained internal skills and tested exit arrangements. Replacement may be limited by specialist knowledge and transition time. **Critical/high, depending on scope.** |
| **Enterprise software, security agents, APIs and software-update infrastructure** — integration, workflow, endpoint protection and business support | Malicious or defective update, vulnerable shared component or compromised integration → correlated outages across production and recovery | Staged deployment, rollback and independently recoverable configurations. A second site running the same affected software may fail identically. **High to critical.** |
| **Telecommunications, internet, DNS, network/security gateways and customer authentication delivery** — connect customers, branches, ATMs, markets and providers | DDoS, routing/DNS manipulation or carrier/network-management compromise → inaccessible services even where applications remain healthy | Physically and operationally diverse carriers, alternate connectivity and independent crisis communications. Separate contracts may still share ducts, exchanges or upstream providers. **Critical.** |
| **Electricity, cooling, building management, physical access and fire systems** — sustain safe operation of data centres, branches and cash facilities | Compromise of utility or facilities controls → loss of power/cooling, inaccessible premises or protective shutdown | UPS, generators, fuel, alternate premises and geographically independent capacity. Backup endurance and supplier replenishment matter. **Critical for hosting sites; variable elsewhere.** |
| **Cash logistics, ATM servicing, secure transport, hardware/spares and specialist personnel** — maintain cash access and repair physical infrastructure | Cyber disruption to dispatch, access, inventory or maintenance systems → cash shortages, unavailable ATMs and delayed recovery | Alternate routes/providers, stocked spares and trained backup staff. Cash is not an unlimited substitute for digital payments and requires its own functioning supply chain. **High for cash-dependent customers and locations.** |



### Documented foundations for the matrix


**Core-data recovery — EU, official exercise, high confidence:** the ECB’s **26 July 2024** cyber-resilience stress-test findings concern a fictitious attack severely affecting core-system databases. Banks were assessed on response and recovery, including critical third-party dependencies. This supports treating data integrity and recoverability as critical; it does not establish an actual compromise at the tested banks. ([bankingsupervision.europa.eu](https://www.bankingsupervision.europa.eu/press/pr/date/2024/html/ssm.pr240726~06d5776a02.hu.html?utm_source=openai))



**Messaging and settlement — UK/global, observed non-cyber outage, high confidence:** Bank of England statistics record a **245-minute CHAPS settlement outage on 18 July 2024**, caused by Swift’s Y-Copy service issue. CREST and retail net settlement were not affected; settlement hours were extended and all transactions submitted to the Bank settled successfully. The event demonstrates a shared-service dependency, not a cyberattack or failure of every settlement service. ([bankofengland.co.uk](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/payment-and-settlement/payment-and-settlement-statistics?trk=public_post_comment-text&utm_source=openai))



**Energy and telecoms — EU, official risk assessment, high confidence:** the European Commission’s **24 July 2024** publication identifies supply-chain risks, ransomware, wipers and zero-day exploitation as concerns in electricity and telecommunications, particularly for OT. These are upstream infrastructure risks; they do not establish that banks operate industrial control systems themselves. ([digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/risk-assessment-report-cyber-resilience-eus-telecommunications-and-electricity-sectors?utm_source=openai))



**Named technology dependencies — global financial-sector research, moderate confidence:** research published **4 November 2025**, based on observable technical connections, identifies financial-sector suppliers including Bloomberg, FactSet, Murex, Fiserv, CyberArk and Entrust. It also identifies building-automation and access-control dependencies. The population includes financial organisations beyond banks, and observable connections do not establish each supplier’s operational criticality for each customer. [1](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2280730163635307544)


## 3. Major single points of failure and service bottlenecks


**Priority assessment — conditional, not confirmed weaknesses at named banks:**

1. **Authoritative records and their recovery chain.** If the ledger, journals and backups share one compromised administrative boundary, spare computing capacity cannot by itself restore trustworthy balances.
2. **Shared identity and management infrastructure.** Production, backup administration, supplier access and incident communications can become unavailable together.
3. **A bundled core or processing provider.** One incident can simultaneously remove account servicing, digital channels, payments and operational support.
4. **A sole settlement-access intermediary.** The bank may remain technically operational but unable to complete transactions in the required currency or market.
5. **Common software across both sites.** A supplier update or exploit can defeat geographic redundancy.
6. **Concentrated specialist capacity.** Recovery can stall on a small group of engineers, database specialists, treasury authorisers or vendor personnel.
7. **Deadline and backlog capacity.** Manual fallbacks can preserve a minimum service but fail to clear transaction queues before settlement, margin, payroll or reporting deadlines.

The strongest continuity evidence is therefore **tested completion of the business service with trustworthy data**, rather than the existence of a second data centre or an alternative supplier contract.



**Documented fallback limitation:** CFTC materials describing the **January 2023 ION ransomware incident** state that affected firms processed trades manually, with consequences for timely and accurate reporting. This demonstrates that a workaround can exist without preserving normal throughput or reporting timeliness. ([cftc.gov](https://www.cftc.gov/LawRegulation/FederalRegister/proposedrules/2023-28745.html?utm_source=openai))


## 4. Cross-sector concentration exposures

| Shared exposure | Documented evidence | Transmission assessment |
|---|---|---|
| **Cloud and underlying ICT providers** | The ESAs’ **23 September 2026** risk update identifies dependence on non-EEA ICT providers and infrastructures as a concern that could amplify operational disruption. US Treasury’s **8 February 2023** cloud assessment also identifies concentration and material data gaps. **High confidence in the identified risk; no bank-level exposure percentages assigned.** ([esma.europa.eu](https://www.esma.europa.eu/press-news/esma-news/esas-call-vigilance-over-external-dependencies-cyber-threats-and-private?utm_source=openai)) | A shared provider can interrupt banks, insurers, payment firms and corporate customers simultaneously. Several direct suppliers may conceal a common underlying host. |
| **Operating systems and security software** | CISA confirmed on **19 July 2024** that the CrowdStrike-related Windows outage arose from a content update, **not malicious cyber activity**. Microsoft’s **20 July** account describes broad economic effects from use of the affected software in critical services. **High confidence; historical non-cyber example.** ([content.govdelivery.com](https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/USDHSCISA/bulletins/3a9a578?utm_source=openai)) | The same distribution pathway could transmit a malicious update, while simultaneous failures in customers or logistics providers could compound the bank’s own interruption. |
| **Core and payment-processing providers** | The Kansas City Fed’s **March 2024** research documents providers operating across core services and adjacent payment markets. **High confidence for the historical structure, not current ownership or market shares.** ([kansascityfed.org](https://www.kansascityfed.org/research/payments-system-research-briefings/market-structure-of-core-banking-services-providers/?utm_source=openai)) | One provider incident could affect multiple banks and the merchants, employers or public bodies whose payments they process. |
| **Shared financial infrastructure and specialist software** | The ION and CHAPS examples demonstrate disruption propagating through shared trade-processing or messaging services. **High confidence in the documented events; different causes and scopes.** ([cftc.gov](https://www.cftc.gov/PressRoom/SpeechesTestimony/cftcstatement020223?utm_source=openai)) | Effects can reach counterparties, settlement agents, corporate treasuries and regulators without compromising their own systems. |
| **Electricity, telecommunications and physical infrastructure** | The EU’s **July 2024** assessment documents cyber risks in these upstream critical sectors. **High confidence in the risk categories.** ([digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/risk-assessment-report-cyber-resilience-eus-telecommunications-and-electricity-sectors?utm_source=openai)) | A regional incident can impair bank sites, carrier networks, data centres, cash access and customer businesses together, limiting otherwise available workarounds. |

## 5. Business-continuity implications and evidence gaps


**UK evidence of realised third-party involvement:** the FCA’s **28 July 2026** publication states that third-party issues accounted for **27% of incidents reported by firms in 2025**, of which **37% were cyber-related**. These figures cover reporting financial firms, not banks alone, and do not measure supplier market concentration. [2](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2281628059314421762)



For credit analysis, continuity depends on whether the institution can demonstrate:

- Independent access to **accurate customer, cash, securities and collateral records**.
- A workable **minimum service** for essential payments, account access and customer communications.
- Recovery arrangements independent of the failed **identity, administrator, supplier and software pathway**.
- Sufficient **manual-processing and reconciliation capacity**, not merely a documented fallback.
- Visibility through direct suppliers to **subcontractors, hosting, telecoms, energy and specialist personnel**.

**Evidence gaps:** public material rarely establishes a bank’s complete supplier chain, physical carrier diversity, backup independence, key-management architecture, cash-logistics concentration or tested recovery throughput. Global evidence is strongest here for common dependency categories; detailed infrastructure and provider evidence is weighted toward the EU, UK and US. No worldwide supplier market shares, institution-specific recovery times, undisclosed weaknesses or financial losses are inferred.

**Overall conclusion:** banking’s principal operational concentration is in the shared digital and financial infrastructure required to authenticate instructions, maintain trustworthy records and complete settlement. The material credit distinction is not simply outsourcing versus insourcing, but whether critical services can continue—or be restored safely—when a common dependency fails.
