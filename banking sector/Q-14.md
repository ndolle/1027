# Banking: governance structure and operating-model exposure
**Evidence cutoff: 27 October 2026 | Worldwide scope, with EU, UK and US distinctions**

## 1. Preliminary assessment


**Inherent governance and operating-model exposure: high for diversified, cross-border and intermediary-intensive banking models; more variable for simpler institutions.**

Banking combines legal-entity accountability with services that cross organisational boundaries. A payment may depend on a local bank, group technology company, external processor, correspondent and infrastructure operator. Responsibility for the customer outcome can therefore extend beyond the organisation that operates each component.

**This is structural complexity—not evidence of ineffective governance.** Centralisation can strengthen standards and expertise; decentralisation can improve local knowledge and responsiveness. Either can work when authority, information, resources and escalation are aligned. Residual risk requires institution-specific evidence.


## 2. Principal governance complexity drivers



| Structural driver | Banking-specific accountability challenge | Conditions increasing materiality | Potential mitigating arrangements |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Holding companies and regulated subsidiaries** | Group strategy and technology decisions may sit above the entity legally responsible for customers, capital or service delivery | Different boards, funding constraints or competing entity priorities | Clear entity responsibilities, enforceable service arrangements and consolidated visibility |
| **Branches and cross-border operations** | One service spans home/host supervision, privacy, reporting, outsourcing and resolution requirements | Restrictions on data access, support, transfers or local recovery | Jurisdiction-specific responsibilities and tested cross-border crisis arrangements |
| **Shared-service companies and global operating centres** | A central team delivers technology, payments, procurement or support for several entities | Local management cannot direct resources or obtain service information | Named service owners, transparent dependencies and entity-level escalation rights |
| **Business-line and regional matrices** | Product, country, technology and risk leaders may each own only part of a process | Decisions require multiple committees; exceptions lack a final owner | Clear decision authority and a single accountable owner for critical-service outcomes |
| **Subsidiaries, joint ventures and minority interests** | Ownership does not necessarily confer operational control or full access to evidence | Shared platforms, brand exposure or support obligations without corresponding rights | Explicit information, audit, incident-cooperation and exit arrangements |
| **Agents, introducers and platform partnerships** | Distribution and customer contact may be separated from underwriting, records, compliance and servicing | High growth, several intermediaries, inconsistent customer records | Defined responsibilities, reconciled records and direct access to critical information |
| **Mergers and legacy integration** | Acquired entities retain different systems, data definitions, suppliers and control processes | Integration runs alongside major cost reductions or additional migrations | Sequenced integration, retained expertise, tested transitions and accountable retirement plans |
| **Outsourcing and subcontracting** | Procurement owns the contract, technology owns integration, and a business unit owns the service | Unknown subcontractors or a supplier supporting several essential functions | Service-level ownership and assurance extending beyond the immediate contract |
| **Incentives and information asymmetry** | Staff or executives possess more information than boards and independent control functions | Aggressive targets, suppression of exceptions or weak challenge | Balanced incentives, independent reporting and effective investigation/escalation |

**Franchise qualification:** conventional franchising is not a universal banking model. Comparable boundary issues arise through agents, white-label distribution, broker networks, correspondent relationships and banking-as-a-service arrangements.


### Variation by subsector


- **Universal/international banks:** entity, product, jurisdiction and shared-infrastructure complexity are prominent.
- **Community/regional banks:** a simpler corporate chart can coexist with substantial outsourced-core and provider dependence.
- **Digital banks/BaaS:** customer-facing technology, regulated deposit-taking and records may sit in different organisations.
- **Custody/transaction banking:** responsibility must span settlement agents, correspondents, subcustodians and deadline-sensitive processing.
- **Investment banking:** front-office activity, risk measurement, collateral, clearing and reporting require consistent ownership across businesses and entities.
- **Cooperative/mutual networks:** central services and member institutions require clear allocation of decision rights; decentralisation is not itself a weakness.


## 3. Why shared technology and critical processes complicate ownership


Five interfaces deserve particular attention:

1. **Service owner versus system owner:** several systems can each meet their targets while the complete payment or servicing process fails.
2. **Group policy versus local accountability:** local management may retain obligations but lack authority over group infrastructure.
3. **Contract owner versus operational dependency:** procurement visibility does not establish that business continuity or exit is workable.
4. **First-line delivery versus independent challenge:** risk and audit can identify issues but cannot substitute for management owning and fixing them.
5. **Normal operations versus crisis authority:** an incident can require rapid decisions on isolation, customer access and liquidity across several entities.

The relevant question is not simply “Who is the CISO?” but **“Who can decide, fund and execute recovery of the whole service—and who independently challenges that decision?”**



Bitsight’s **November 2025** research illustrates the breadth of external interfaces: it analysed **41,511 financial organisations** and **50,232 identified technology-provider relationships**, including specialist finance, identity, legacy-support and facilities suppliers. These are observable relationships across financial services—not a complete organisational map or a banking governance-failure count. **Important counterexample:** a disruption alone does not prove governance failure. The previously reviewed ION incident demonstrated processing dependence, but workarounds supported obligations without defaults. Evidence of impact and evidence of ineffective oversight must remain separate.


## 5. Supervisory findings and governance benchmarks

### Board capability, information and accountability


A **26 May 2026 BIS-hosted speech** explains that the Basel Committee’s 2015 governance principles emphasise board responsibility and risk appetite, while individual-accountability regimes formalise senior responsibilities and reasonable-step expectations.

It also warns that formal board independence is insufficient without information, time, support and banking expertise. The speech states that research does not establish a clear relationship between board size/independence/diversity and reduced bank failure probability. These are the speaker’s policy assessments—not new binding rules or a sector failure rate. [7](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2281482229588688896)


### Recovery, contracts and risk-sensitive investment


The ECB’s **July 2024 exercise** assessed cyber response/recovery at **109 banks**, including enhanced testing at **28**, and identified improvement needs. Its **December 2024 supervisory priorities** also reported non-compliance in more than **10% of contracts covering critical functions** and concentration concerns. These findings concern the assessed populations, not all worldwide banks. [8](https://www.bankingsupervision.europa.eu/press/pr/date/2024/html/ssm.pr240726~06d5776a02.hu.html) [9](https://www.bankingsupervision.europa.eu/framework/priorities/pdf/ssm.supervisory_priorities202412~6f69ad032f.en.pdf)



A **26 May 2026 BIS working paper**, using confidential ECB supervisory data, identifies banks underinvesting relative to their cyber-risk profiles and examines supervisory incentives. It supports heterogeneity in preparedness—not a worldwide underinvestment percentage or the conclusion that spending alone measures governance. [10](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2281483054222410654)



**Jurisdictional implications:**
- **EU:** group governance and DORA-related ICT responsibilities require a clear management-body and entity-level allocation.
- **UK:** senior accountability, important-business-service ownership and outsourcing expectations make authority and responsibility alignment material.
- **US:** charter-based supervision and subsidiary/activity-specific rules require oversight beyond a consolidated group policy.

Basel principles, explanatory guidance and research should not be presented as automatically binding legislation. Legal applicability requires entity-specific confirmation.


## 6. Comparable indicators across sectors


The strongest retrieved common-dataset comparison is narrow: Bitsight financial-sector customers monitored an average **36.3%** of inferred supply chains, compared with **24.6%** for other organisations. The report notes that unmonitored suppliers may have been deemed non-critical. This is a measure of observed monitoring—not a complete governance comparison. [1](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2280730163635307544)



**No defensible worldwide cross-sector governance-complexity ranking was established.** Raw subsidiary, country or supplier counts are confounded by firm size and business mix.

For comparable borrower analysis, use consistent definitions and normalise where appropriate:

| Structural indicator | What to assess alongside it |
|---|---|
| Regulated entities and jurisdictions | Material activities, conflicting obligations and entity decision rights |
| Critical services spanning multiple entities | Presence of a single accountable outcome owner |
| Outsourcing/subcontracting layers | Visibility, audit access and executable incident cooperation |
| Legacy platforms and incomplete integrations | Retirement milestones, control consistency and reconciled data |
| Shared-service concentration | Local authority, service transparency and tested alternatives |
| Governance interfaces/committees | Decision speed, escalation effectiveness and unresolved ownership |
| Repeated or overdue findings | Risk acceptance, funding, accountability and independent retesting |

These are proposed assessment indicators—not published sector-average thresholds. High structural counts are not automatically adverse; repeat unresolved failures are more direct evidence of governance performance.


## 7. Operational-risk implications and final judgement


Governance complexity can transmit into operational risk through delayed decisions, incomplete aggregation, contradictory recovery priorities, unowned exceptions and supplier changes that no business owner fully understands. Financial consequences can include prolonged interruption, fraud losses, remediation expense, regulatory action and liquidity pressure.

**Materiality is greater where responsibility exceeds authority:** for example, an entity is accountable for settlement but cannot obtain records, direct the service provider or access recovery resources. It is lower where end-to-end ownership, independent challenge and tested cooperation bridge organisational boundaries.

**Preliminary inherent assessment:** banking warrants heightened scrutiny of accountability interfaces, especially in cross-border universal groups, acquisition-heavy organisations and intermediary-intensive platforms. Simpler institutions may have less corporate complexity but remain highly dependent on external operations.

**Confidence**
- **High:** structural mechanisms, the cited OCC finding and BIS discussion of board/accountability requirements.
- **Moderate:** generalisation from supervisory samples and secondary TSB/BaaS accounts.
- **Limited:** worldwide prevalence, cross-sector rankings and individual-borrower governance effectiveness.

**Priority validation gaps:** current entity/service maps; intragroup and supplier decision rights; post-acquisition integration status; original enforcement documents; data ownership; and evidence that repeat findings are resolved. No quantified BCBS 239 compliance benchmark is used because a sufficiently verified underlying report was not retrieved.

**Bottom line:** complexity increases the demands on governance; it does not determine its quality. Assess whether the institution can identify an accountable owner, deliver reliable information, make timely decisions and demonstrate execution across its organisational boundaries.
