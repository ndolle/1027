# Banking: cross-sector inherent non-financial risk comparison
**Evidence cutoff: 27 October 2026 | Scope: worldwide, with EU, UK and US qualifications**

## Executive conclusion


Banking has distinctive exposure to **transferable money, transaction integrity, settlement deadlines and interconnected financial obligations**. These characteristics support elevated concern across fraud, ICT and operational dependencies—but do not justify calling banking the most exposed sector overall.

Compared with relevant peers:

- **Fraud:** banking has greater direct exposure to payment execution, account misuse and fraudulent credit representations. Retail, construction and manufacturing have different concentrations in merchandise, procurement and project fraud.
- **Cyber/ICT:** banking is highly digitally dependent, but generally has less industrial-process and physical-safety exposure than energy, manufacturing and healthcare.
- **Operational resilience/TPRM:** payment and settlement interconnections create distinctive propagation and liquidity channels. Healthcare, energy, logistics and ICT providers have equally important—but different—systemic dependencies.
- **Governance:** cross-border universal banks can be exceptionally complex; a small domestic bank need not be more complex than a multinational manufacturer, hospital group or platform business.

**These are structural assessments, not residual-risk rankings.** Available benchmarks provide partial comparisons of reported incidents, costs or monitoring—not a common four-dimensional risk score.


## 1. Comparative sector exposure matrix



| Dimension | Objective structural indicators | Banking versus relevant peers | Assessment and boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fraud** | Payment volume/value; transferable assets under authority; credit decisions based on external representations; intermediaries; employee approval/custody powers | More direct exposure than ordinary non-financial businesses to customer-payment fraud and credit-document manipulation. Retail has merchandise/card-acquiring exposure; construction and industrial businesses have substantial supplier/project interfaces; insurance has claims-specific exposure | **High inherent banking exposure**, but no validated population-adjusted cross-sector fraud-loss ranking |
| **Cyber / ICT** | Critical-service digitisation; internet/API exposure; identity and data concentration; transaction deadlines; OT dependence; recovery independence | Banking depends heavily on digital records and remote services. Energy/manufacturing have greater industrial-control exposure; healthcare adds clinical/safety consequences; technology/telecom firms can be upstream providers to all | Banking is **highly exposed, not demonstrably highest**. Different harm types cannot be reduced to one incident count |
| **Operational Resilience / TPRM** | Critical-provider concentration; functions per provider; settlement interconnections; fallback throughput; maximum tolerable interruption; switching constraints | Banking can transmit operational failure through payments, collateral and liquidity. Healthcare has clinical-service dependencies; manufacturing has production/logistics bottlenecks; ICT providers create broad customer dependence | **High sensitivity to common dependencies**. Relative financial consequences depend on duration and alternatives |
| **Governance / Operating Model** | Material legal entities/jurisdictions; shared-service interfaces; subcontracting layers; integration programmes; ownership of end-to-end services | Universal banks combine regulated entities, shared platforms and local accountability. Global industrial, energy and technology groups can be comparably complex. Smaller banks may have simpler structures but concentrated outsourcing | **High for complex banking models; variable across the sector.** Complexity is not proof of ineffective oversight |



## 2. Comparable quantitative benchmarks

### A. Breach costs: common methodology, conditional on experiencing a breach


IBM/Ponemon’s **2025 study** provides the following nominal **US-dollar mean costs per studied breach**:

| Broad industry | Mean breach cost |
|---|---:|
| Healthcare | **US$7.42m** |
| Financial | **US$5.56m** |
| Industrial | **US$5.00m** |
| Energy | **US$4.83m** |
| Technology | **US$4.79m** |
| Professional services | **US$4.56m** |
| Retail | **US$3.54m** |

The study covered **600 breached organisations**, **17 industries**, **16 countries/regions**, and breaches occurring **March 2024–February 2025**. It used **3,470 interviews** and activity-based estimates of direct, indirect and lost-business costs. The financial category includes **banks, insurers and investment companies**; industrial includes chemical processing, engineering and manufacturing.

Industry sample shares were rounded: financial **14%**, industrial **12%**, services **11%**, technology **10%**, energy **8%**, retail **5%** and healthcare **2%**. These are not exact sector counts. [1](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2280503664239983616)



**Interpretation:** financial firms had higher reported mean costs than the listed industrial, energy, technology, services and retail samples, but lower than healthcare. This compares **cost conditional on a studied breach**, not inherent exposure or expected annual loss.

No published size-, geography- and breach-scale-adjusted sector estimates were available. The sample is nonstatistical, excludes very small and very large breaches, changes annually and does not support conventional confidence intervals. A bank-only or median-cost comparison is therefore unavailable.


### B. Ransomware: common survey with a defined size range


Secondary reporting of Sophos’s **2024 survey** gives:

| Measure | Financial services | Comparator |
|---|---:|---:|
| Respondents reporting ransomware in the preceding year | **65%** | **59% overall** |
| Mean share of computers affected within victim organisations | **43%** | **49% overall** |
| Mean share of computers affected—selected peers | — | **62% energy/oil/gas/utilities; 58% healthcare** |

Fieldwork was **January–February 2024**, covering **5,000 IT/cybersecurity leaders**, including **592 financial-services respondents**, across **14 countries**. All represented organisations with **100–5,000 employees**. [2](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2279368171842437123)



**Interpretation:** the same survey suggests higher reported victimisation but narrower device impact in financial services. This does not prove better containment: business mix, attack type, detection and sampling can contribute.

The common size band improves comparability but does not equal size matching. Sector-specific country/size distributions, weighting and uncertainty were not established. The overall comparator includes financial services, and device impact is not service downtime or financial loss. Confidence is **moderate**, given reliance on secondary reporting.


### C. Third-party monitoring: comparable practice, not inherent risk


Bitsight’s **November 2025** original research found financial-sector customers monitored an average **36.3% of their inferred supply chains**, compared with **24.6% for other organisations**.

The monitoring analysis involved nearly **600 financial-sector customers**, collectively monitoring over **46,000 organisations**. The broader dependency analysis covered **41,511 financial organisations** and **50,232 identified technology-provider relationships**. Suppliers were inferred using observable technical connections and related data.

The report also states that one comparison showed **no clear security-performance difference between financial and other sectors**. [3](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2280730163635307544)



**Interpretation:** monitoring coverage was higher in this financial-sector customer population. It does not establish superior recovery or lower inherent concentration. The metric is proportion-based, but is not adjusted for customer size, supplier criticality or geography. Monitoring outside the vendor platform and justified exclusion of non-critical suppliers can affect results.


### D. Fraud and governance: comparison gaps


Secondary coverage of ACFE’s **2026** study reports investigated case counts of **439 in banking/financial services**, **193 in manufacturing**, **140 in healthcare** and **110 in retail**. The original industry table, corresponding medians and exposed organisation populations were not validated. [4](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2281755649354236002)



These case counts are **not usable incidence rankings**: investigated cases depend on detection, investigator participation, sector size and case selection. No validated common dataset established comparable sector fraud loss rates or governance-complexity scores.

An all-industry estimate of revenue lost to fraud should not be applied to bank revenue. Board composition, certifications or number of subsidiaries likewise cannot serve alone as governance-effectiveness scores.


## 3. Peer-sector interpretation



| Peer sector | Banking is relatively more exposed to… | Banking is relatively less exposed to—or differs from… |
|---|---|---|
| **Healthcare** | Settlement, collateral and payment-system transmission | Direct clinical-service and patient-safety consequences; its higher observed breach cost is not proof of higher attack probability |
| **Manufacturing** | Transferable financial assets, financial records and interbank obligations | Industrial control, production-line interruption, physical inventory and plant restart |
| **Energy/utilities** | Customer financial fraud and financial-market processing | Continuous physical supply, process safety and geographically constrained industrial infrastructure |
| **Retail** | Credit underwriting, custody of funds and settlement/treasury complexity | Merchandise shrinkage and broad physical distribution; both face identity, payment and supplier fraud |
| **Technology/telecoms** | Financial authorisation and prudential consequences | Upstream infrastructure provision to many sectors; technology providers can have larger observable attack surfaces |
| **Professional services** | Transaction execution, balance-sheet credit risk and payment-system connections | Less direct safety/production exposure in both; professional firms can still hold highly sensitive information and influence client payments |

These are directional mechanism comparisons, not measured “more risky overall” conclusions. Supplier disruption, procurement fraud and management override occur across all these sectors.


## 4. Subsector differences that broad classifications conceal


- **Universal banks:** high organisational and cross-border complexity, but potentially greater specialist resources.
- **Digital banks:** high remote-channel dependence; not necessarily high legacy-system exposure.
- **Community/regional banks:** simpler structures can coexist with concentrated core-provider reliance.
- **Custody/transaction banks:** settlement integrity and intraday liquidity can dominate customer-channel availability.
- **Investment banks:** trading, margin and positions create deadline-sensitive dependencies.
- **Specialist lenders:** origination/servicing and funding interfaces may matter more than deposit access.
- **BaaS/sponsor models:** responsibility and records span several organisations.

“Financial services,” “finance and insurance,” and “banking” are not interchangeable categories. Comparisons also mix listed groups, regulated entities and operating establishments unless explicitly controlled.


## 5. Normalisation and methodological limits


**What can be compared:** within-study metrics using the same definitions, period and collection method—subject to stated population differences.

**What cannot currently be normalised reliably:**

| Dimension | Preferred denominator or matching approach | Missing evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Fraud | Same fraud type; net/gross basis; losses per transaction/account or comparable activity | Common sector populations, recovery allocation and consistent liability |
| Cyber/ICT | Matched size, geography, digital footprint and reporting thresholds | Bank-only samples and published adjusted rates |
| Operational/TPRM | Critical services affected, dependency concentration and lost-service hours | Comparable provider/workload shares and restoration definitions |
| Governance | Size/business-model-matched entities, interfaces and jurisdictions | Common definitions and validated links to outcomes |

No numerical correction is applied where microdata are unavailable. Revenue normalisation is especially problematic because banking income, transaction value and industrial sales have different meanings.

**Reporting biases:** stronger detection can increase observed incidents; regulation can increase disclosure; vendor/insurance datasets reflect customer selection; breach costs exclude organisations without breaches. A high count can coexist with effective containment, and low disclosure can coexist with poor preparedness.


## 6. Confidence and decision-useful conclusion


- **High confidence:** banking’s distinctive transaction-integrity, financial-asset and settlement exposure; the cited original IBM values and Bitsight observations.
- **Moderate confidence:** directional peer comparisons and the Sophos survey comparison.
- **Low confidence:** worldwide adjusted incident rankings, fraud-loss rankings, banking workload concentration and cross-sector governance scores.

**Conclusion:** banking warrants heightened attention to payment integrity, common technology dependencies, trustworthy recovery and accountability across entities/providers. Energy, healthcare and manufacturing warrant different emphases around physical continuity and safety. The evidence supports these **different exposure profiles**, not an artificial league table.

For an individual borrower, assess demonstrated controls and financial capacity separately from sector structure. The most useful next evidence is a matched peer population, bank-only denominators, critical-service concentration and tested recovery outcomes.
