# Banking: regulatory authorities and cyber resilience expectations

## 1. Executive comparison


**The principal regulatory difference is structural:**

- **EU:** DORA provides a comparatively harmonised, binding financial-sector ICT resilience framework, supplemented by privacy, payments and prudential requirements.
- **UK:** binding operational-resilience rules emphasise important business services and impact tolerances, alongside outsourcing requirements and direct oversight of designated critical suppliers.
- **US:** obligations are layered across charter-based prudential supervision, information-security requirements, incident notification, securities rules and state law.

**Credit relevance:** the greatest exposures are not limited to fines. Regulatory remediation, restrictions on activities, supplier replacement, customer compensation and impaired operational capacity can affect earnings, liquidity and franchise strength.


## 2. European Union

### Authorities and applicability


**Authority map:** within the Banking Union, the ECB directly supervises significant banks and national competent authorities supervise less significant banks within the Single Supervisory Mechanism. National authorities also retain responsibilities outside the ECB’s remit. The EBA develops banking standards and guidance; it is not the routine direct supervisor of every EU bank. The three European Supervisory Authorities—EBA, ESMA and EIOPA—participate in DORA’s critical ICT-provider oversight. National data-protection authorities enforce GDPR.

Examples of relevant national authorities include BaFin in Germany, ACPR in France, the Central Bank of Ireland, DNB/AFM in the Netherlands and CSSF in Luxembourg. The competent authority and reporting channel depend on the entity and activity.



**Branch distinction:** CSSF’s **27 August 2026** publication addresses DORA’s application to third-country branches in Luxembourg, following a Commission clarification. A foreign-headquartered group should therefore not assume that operating through a branch removes DORA obligations; the relevant branch and home-entity classification require examination. [1](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2281696034352529415)


### Principal EU frameworks

| Framework and status | Scope and principal expectations | Reporting, timing and consequences |
|---|---|---|
| **DORA—Regulation (EU) 2022/2554 and applicable technical standards: binding law** | Applies from **17 January 2025** to financial entities within its statutory scope. Covers ICT governance/risk management, incident management, resilience testing and ICT third-party risk. Scope includes banks and specified investment/payment entities, with entity-specific exclusions and proportionality. [2](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2280055719552090160) | Supervision can require remediation and use applicable investigative, administrative and sanctioning powers. Do not apply GDPR’s fine ceilings to DORA or assume one uniform national penalty schedule. |
| **DORA major-incident reporting: binding requirements** | Requires incident classification and reporting of **major ICT-related incidents**, rather than notification of every security event. Retrieved legal/regulatory commentary describes an initial notification within **four hours of major classification**, subject to the **24-hour awareness limit**, followed by an intermediate report within **72 hours of initial notification** and a final report within **one month of the intermediate report**. [3](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2281760345162776595) | The technical standards contain qualifications, including treatment of later classification and updated intermediate reports. The exact trigger and applicable reporting calendar must be implemented from the governing standards—not reduced to “24 hours after any attack.” |
| **DORA testing and recovery: binding, proportionate obligations** | Requires resilience testing and continuity arrangements. Selected institutions face advanced threat-led penetration testing; this is not a universal requirement for every financial entity. [4](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2281548648355463176) | Recovery evidence should cover critical services, data integrity and dependencies. There is no single DORA recovery-time objective applicable to every bank application. |
| **DORA ICT third-party risk and critical-provider oversight: binding** | Includes dependency/register requirements, concentration management and oversight of designated critical providers. Outsourcing does not transfer the financial entity’s responsibility. [5](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2281812834058764288) | Contracts, access/audit rights, incident cooperation, continuity and viable exit arrangements are material compliance and credit issues. Designation of a provider does not certify every customer arrangement as resilient. |
| **GDPR: binding privacy law** | Applies independently to relevant personal-data processing. A controller generally notifies the competent data-protection authority within **72 hours of awareness**, where feasible, unless the breach is unlikely to risk individuals’ rights and freedoms. High-risk breaches also require communication to affected individuals. [6](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2281746112379365206) | A DORA report does not automatically discharge GDPR obligations. Security, processor oversight, compensation exposure and privacy enforcement can remain relevant even without a banking outage. |
| **NIS2 and national implementing law: binding where applicable** | DORA operates as the more specific regime for relevant financial-sector obligations. This is **not a blanket exemption for every group company or supplier**; mixed groups and ICT providers can have separate NIS2 exposure. [7](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2281819313721573383) | National implementation, competent authorities and group-entity classification must be checked separately. |
| **Payments and prudential requirements: binding rules plus supervisory guidance** | Payment authentication, fraud controls, governance, outsourcing and safe operation continue alongside DORA. PSD2-derived requirements and relevant authentication standards concern payment activities; they are not substitutes for enterprise-wide resilience. EBA guidance and ECB expectations must be distinguished from legislation and binding supervisory decisions. | A payment institution, bank, investment subsidiary and ordinary nonbank lender should not be assigned identical obligations solely because they share a parent. |

### Supervisory priorities and developments


The ECB’s **2024 cyber stress test** covered 109 banks and identified improvement needs in response/recovery, critical third-party dependencies and impact assessment. Its **December 2024 supervisory priorities** also identified shortcomings in contracts for critical functions and provider/geographic concentration. These are supervisory findings—not evidence that every bank failed the same control. [8](https://www.bankingsupervision.europa.eu/press/pr/date/2024/html/ssm.pr240726~06d5776a02.hu.html) [9](https://www.bankingsupervision.europa.eu/framework/priorities/pdf/ssm.supervisory_priorities202412~6f69ad032f.en.pdf)



**2026 developments:** July legal analysis describes ECB expectations for significant institutions to address AI-enabled threats and outstanding ICT findings. October analysis reports final EBA third-party-risk guidelines extending to non-ICT services alongside DORA; the application date was not fixed in the retrieved **20 October** account. These should not be treated as an already-operative replacement timetable without checking the final instrument. [10](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2281586590901862416) [11](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2281822166217392130)


## 3. United Kingdom

### Authorities and principal obligations


**Authority map:** the PRA supervises prudential safety and soundness of banks, building societies and relevant investment firms; the FCA oversees conduct and applicable systems/controls. The Bank of England supervises relevant financial-market infrastructures. The ICO enforces data-protection law. HM Treasury designates critical third parties for the joint financial-sector oversight regime.

A bank’s UK authorisation, permissions and branches matter. EU DORA is not automatically UK domestic law, although an EU entity in the same group may be subject to it.


| Framework and status | Applicable obligations and scope | Timing and implications |
|---|---|---|
| **FCA/PRA operational-resilience rules: binding; SS1/21 explains PRA expectations** | For in-scope firms: identify important business services, set impact tolerances, map dependencies, test severe-but-plausible disruption and remediate vulnerabilities. The transition to demonstrating the ability to remain within tolerances ended **31 March 2025**. Applicability is not identical across banks, credit unions, payment firms and investment businesses. | This is an ongoing outcome obligation, not a one-off compliance project. Impact tolerance is not the same as an individual application’s recovery target. |
| **Outsourcing and third-party requirements; PRA SS2/21: rules plus supervisory expectations** | Assess materiality, due diligence, data security, audit/access rights, continuity, concentration and exit. Material arrangements and changes may require advance supervisory engagement/notification. Management remains accountable. | Supplier failure, weak access rights or an impractical exit can drive remediation, restrictions and replacement expenditure. |
| **Existing regulatory incident reporting: binding rules/principles** | FCA Principle 11/SUP 15 and PRA openness/cooperation requirements can require prompt notification of significant matters. Payment/e-money and FMI activities can have additional reporting routes. There is no single current deadline that safely replaces every applicable UK obligation. | Do not wait for a complete forensic investigation or assume a GDPR notification also satisfies the financial regulator. |
| **New unified operational incident framework: future commencement** | Retrieved July 2026 legal reporting states that the new framework takes effect **18 March 2027**, with reporting based on defined operational incidents and harm/stability/safety thresholds. [12](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2281629908616609812) | At the research cutoff it is a preparation requirement, not a reason to stop existing notifications. Final rules should govern implementation. |
| **Critical third-party regime: binding on designated providers** | The FCA’s **28 July 2026** publication confirms joint Bank/PRA/FCA oversight of designated providers. It complements—not replaces—firms’ responsibilities for their own outsourcing and resilience. [13](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2281628059314421762) | A provider’s designation is not blanket assurance or permission for a bank to reduce its own controls. |
| **UK GDPR/Data Protection Act: binding** | Risk-appropriate security, processor oversight and personal-breach response apply. The familiar supervisory notification standard is **72 hours from awareness**, where feasible, subject to the risk threshold; high-risk cases require affected-person communication. | Retrieved legal analysis stresses appropriate security under Article 32, including responsibility where services are outsourced. [14](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2280332770158837760) |


**UK credit significance:** service-level obligations make untested workarounds, unrealistic recovery assumptions and supplier dependence particularly important. Supervisory consequences can include required remediation, skilled-person reviews, activity restrictions and enforcement. These are possible powers and responses—not automatic outcomes following every outage.

The detailed foundational UK rules were not fully reproduced in the retrieved material. The scope and commencement of each firm’s applicable Handbook/Rulebook provisions should therefore be verified before using this comparison as a filing calendar.


## 4. United States

### Charter and activity determine the regulator


- **OCC:** national banks, federal savings associations and relevant federal branches/agencies.
- **Federal Reserve:** state member banks, bank holding companies and relevant foreign-bank operations.
- **FDIC:** state nonmember insured banks, alongside state supervisors.
- **NCUA:** federal credit unions and relevant requirements for federally insured credit unions.
- **State authorities:** charter/licensing supervision and applicable security, privacy and breach-notification law.
- **SEC/FINRA/CFTC:** additional obligations for listed issuers, broker-dealers, investment/advisory and derivatives activities.
- **FTC:** relevant nonbank financial institutions under its jurisdiction—not a substitute for bank prudential supervision.


### Principal US frameworks and reporting clocks

| Framework and status | Scope and obligations | Reporting/deadline distinction |
|---|---|---|
| **GLBA-derived information-security standards and banking rules: binding** | Applicable institutions need a risk-based security programme, governance, safeguards, testing, service-provider oversight and response arrangements. FFIEC handbooks and authentication guidance inform supervision but are not themselves new statutes. | Bank/customer notice duties and other regulatory clocks must be assessed separately. |
| **Federal banking agencies’ computer-security notification rule: binding** | Banking organisations notify the appropriate regulator as soon as possible and within **36 hours after determining that a qualifying notification incident occurred**. It is not a 36-hour rule for every alert or every discovery. Covered service providers have a separate obligation to notify affected banking customers of qualifying material service disruption lasting, or reasonably likely to last, **four or more hours**. | The four-hour measure is a disruption threshold—not permission to delay supplier communication for four hours. These precise triggers should be checked against the institution’s governing rule; the original rule text was not retrieved here. |
| **NCUA cyber notification: binding** | Federally insured credit unions have a **72-hour** reportable-cyber-incident notification requirement, effective **1 September 2023**. [15](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2278306840007671809) | Use NCUA’s reportable-incident and reasonable-belief triggers, including applicable third-party notifications—not the bank rule’s determination test. |
| **SEC public-company cyber disclosure: binding** | Material cybersecurity incidents generally require Form 8-K disclosure within **four business days after materiality determination**, not discovery. [16](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2281571328114819073) | A listed holding company can have public disclosure obligations alongside confidential bank-supervisor notices. Materiality assessment cannot simply be deferred until all costs are known. |
| **Amended Regulation S-P: binding for covered securities entities** | Relevant to covered broker-dealers, advisers, funds and transfer agents—not automatically every bank. Smaller covered entities’ compliance deadline was **3 June 2026**. [17](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2281472843927519237) | Incident-response, service-provider and affected-customer notification provisions require a separate securities-entity workflow. Do not substitute the public-company 8-K deadline. |
| **FTC Safeguards Rule: binding for covered nonbanks** | Potentially relevant to nonbank lending and other financial activities within FTC jurisdiction. A covered notification event involving unauthorised acquisition of unencrypted information of at least **500 consumers** can trigger FTC notice within **30 days of discovery**. | This is not a universal banking notification rule. Exemptions, regulator allocation and customer/state notices require separate analysis. |
| **NYDFS 23 NYCRR Part 500: binding state regulation** | Applies by DFS authorisation under relevant New York financial laws; covers more than banks. Requires risk-based programmes and applicable governance, access, vulnerability, incident-response and continuity controls. Class A and smaller/exempt entities have different obligations. Reportable incidents require notice within **72 hours of determination**. [18](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2279651948267307009) [19](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2280697178286469783) | Separate extortion-payment reporting, annual submissions and phased control requirements must also be checked in the current regulation. A New York customer alone does not establish Part 500 licensing scope. |
| **Other state privacy/breach laws: binding where applicable** | State requirements can add notice obligations to the federal framework. [20](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2281306781298720769) | Exemptions may be entity-based or data-based. GLBA treatment under one state privacy law does not establish exemption from all state breach laws. |
| **CIRCIA: enacted framework; implementation pending at cutoff** | October 2026 legal analysis states that reporting obligations were not yet effective and awaited final coverage, thresholds, procedures and commencement. [21](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2281796689914242920) | Do not present anticipated CIRCIA reporting clocks as already-operative requirements or assume that existing financial-regulator reporting automatically provides an exemption. |

### Recent US supervisory developments


- **NYDFS, September 2026:** guidance highlights incomplete asset inventories, inconsistent risk-assessment methods, weak governance and poor integration of assessments into control decisions. The guidance explains existing Part 500 expectations rather than creating a new law. [22](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2281759999770230794)
- **Federal banking third-party guidance, September 2026:** retrieved legal analysis describes **proposed** changes. They should not be represented as an already-effective replacement for existing guidance. [23](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2281762925381156864)
- **OCC, September 2026:** the updated cybersecurity work programme aligned with NIST categories but did not establish new regulatory expectations or require banks to use that assessment tool. [24](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2281756243301236738)


## 5. Enforcement evidence and credit consequences


**Direct bank example—Capital One:** the OCC imposed an **US$80m penalty in August 2020** for cloud-risk assessment and remediation deficiencies. The action demonstrates that governance and delayed correction can create financial liability, not merely a technical finding. [25](https://www.occ.gov/news-issuances/news-releases/2020/nr-occ-2020-101.html)



**Adjacent financial-sector example—Delta Dental:** NYDFS’s **30 April 2026** primary announcement records a **US$2.25m** settlement, including cybersecurity-program and reporting failings. This is an insurer example, not a bank penalty, but illustrates Part 500 enforcement across its broader perimeter. [26](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2281421764938432512)



No verified DORA bank penalty or UK cyber-specific enforcement amount is added merely to populate the comparison. Older operational-resilience actions must also be distinguished from cyberattacks.

**Most credit-relevant requirements:**

| Requirement | Credit transmission |
|---|---|
| Service continuity and tested recovery | Restoration expenditure, lost fees, customer harm, settlement and liquidity pressure |
| Supplier oversight and exit | Replacement costs, migration risk, concentration and limits on outsourcing strategy |
| Prompt incident classification/reporting | Parallel investigations, remediation and disclosure exposure if processes fail |
| Privacy/data protection | Notification, customer protection, litigation and regulatory liabilities even without downtime |
| Governance and remediation | Sustained expense, management burden and possible restrictions on activities or expansion |
| Payment and transaction safeguards | Fraud losses, compensation, reconciliation and confidence effects |

Fines are not automatic, and an incident does not by itself prove a rule breach. Conversely, continued service availability does not eliminate privacy or governance liability.


## 6. Applicability-based checklist


Before concluding that a borrower complies, establish:

1. **Legal-entity perimeter:** bank, branch, building society, credit union, payment/e-money firm, broker-dealer, adviser, nonbank lender or ICT supplier.
2. **Authorities and licences:** prudential, conduct, privacy, securities, state/national and infrastructure supervision.
3. **Status of each instrument:** legislation, binding rule, technical standard, supervisory guidance, proposal or voluntary framework.
4. **Parallel notification matrix:** awareness, determination, materiality, major classification and reasonable belief; recipient, deadline, updates and customer communication.
5. **Critical-service evidence:** service owners, impact tolerances, recovery objectives, integrity validation and backlog capacity.
6. **Supplier evidence:** materiality, access/audit rights, subcontractors, incident cooperation, testing, concentration and executable exit.
7. **Subsector safeguards:** payment authorisation, client assets, custody records, settlement, margin/collateral and cash operations.
8. **Outstanding findings:** scope, remediation dates, repeat issues, independent retesting and enforcement restrictions.
9. **Financial provision:** gross expenditure, possible compensation/fines, insurance timing, liquidity and capital headroom.
10. **Change calendar:** distinguish already-effective obligations from the UK 2027 framework, pending CIRCIA implementation and proposed guidance.

**Industry standards versus law:** NIST CSF, Basel principles, ISO standards and CRI profiles provide useful assurance structures but are not automatically binding legislation. SWIFT, card-network, exchange and clearing requirements may be contractually or participation-based binding within their scope; they do not certify whole-bank resilience.

**Confidence:** high in the broad jurisdictional architecture and cited primary findings; moderate where exact implementation details rely on secondary legal reporting. National penalties, exemptions, reporting qualifications and future commencement provisions require entity-specific verification against current legal texts.
