# Banking: cyber resilience capabilities and control benchmarks
**Assessment date: 27 October 2026 | Scope: worldwide, with EU, UK and US distinctions**

## 1. Executive assessment


**The most important benchmark is demonstrable delivery of critical banking services through disruption—not ownership of particular security products.** A resilient institution must protect identities and transaction integrity, contain compromise, and restore trustworthy services within justified tolerances.

Priorities should reflect the institution’s activities and dependencies. A custody bank, digital-only retail bank and small bank using an outsourced core need comparable assurance over critical outcomes, but not identical technology, staffing or architecture.



FS-ISAC’s *Fundamentals of Operational Resilience* supports this proportionate approach: identify critical operations and dependencies, establish risk-based protection, develop response plans, exercise them and maintain governance. It explicitly recommends customisation for an organisation’s size, complexity and role in the financial ecosystem. The retrieved document bears a 2023 edition label and was indexed in June 2024. [1](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2279293582974124032)


## 2. Prioritised sector resilience capabilities


The following is an **assessment framework and recommended evidence standard**, not a universal legal checklist. Priority 1 capabilities protect the ability to transact and recover; Priority 2 capabilities strengthen containment and response. All may be essential in a particular operating model.

| Priority / capability | Critical process protected and attack pathway mitigated | Evidence of effective capability | Consequence if ineffective |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1 — Critical-service ownership, mapping and governance** | Payments, account access, lending, custody, trading and settlement; addresses hidden dependencies and poor recovery prioritisation | Named service owners; mapping to systems, people, data, facilities and suppliers; justified disruption tolerances; funded remediation | A technically successful recovery may leave the actual service unusable or miss a settlement deadline |
| **1 — Identity, MFA and privileged-access protection** | Customer access, administration, payment authorisation and supplier access; mitigates stolen credentials, account-recovery abuse and privilege escalation | Risk-appropriate strong authentication; secure enrolment/reset; controlled emergency access; least privilege; service-account governance; prompt revocation and tested monitoring | Account takeover, unauthorised instructions, cloud-data theft or compromise of both production and recovery |
| **1 — Asset visibility, secure configuration and vulnerability management** | Internet services, core systems, APIs, cloud and remote access; mitigates exploitation and configuration errors | Inventories reconciled with discovery; owners and support status; exposure- and exploitability-based prioritisation; verified remediation and exceptions | Unmanaged entry points, persistent exposure, unsafe emergency changes or unknown recovery dependencies |
| **1 — Transaction and data integrity** | Ledgers, payments, positions, collateral and reconciliation; mitigates payment manipulation and corrupted or ambiguous records | Independent approval for high-consequence actions; payment anomaly detection; audit trails; duplicate suppression; reconciliation and data-quality tests | Fraud, incorrect balances, settlement failures or inability to establish which transactions completed |
| **1 — Backup integrity and independently accessible recovery** | Restoration of core records, configurations, identity and essential applications | Protected copies; separation of backup administration; recoverable keys/configuration; clean restoration tests; evidence of data correctness | Available backups may be unusable, compromised or inaccessible; restoration may reproduce corruption |
| **1 — End-to-end continuity and recovery testing** | Minimum banking services, settlement, customer communications and backlog clearance | Severe-but-plausible exercises; technical restoration plus business validation; supplier participation; measured throughput and closed findings | Failover succeeds technically but essential transactions cannot be completed safely or on time |
| **1 — Critical third-party resilience** | Outsourced core processing, cloud, clearing, SaaS, communications and support | Service-specific assurance; subcontractor visibility; restricted access; tested joint response, recovery and exit arrangements | One supplier incident affects multiple functions or institutions; alternatives exist only on paper |
| **2 — Segmentation and containment** | Separation of customer channels, administrative systems, payment environments, management planes and recovery | Tested restrictions on lateral movement; segregated privileged paths; controlled supplier connectivity; rapid isolation procedures | A local compromise spreads or containment requires shutting down otherwise unaffected services |
| **2 — Security monitoring and detection** | Early discovery of identity, endpoint, cloud, network and payment abuse | Coverage of critical assets and interfaces; useful logs; tested detections; actionable escalation; monitoring of privileged changes and bulk extraction | Valid-account abuse or data theft continues unnoticed; evidence is insufficient for safe recovery |
| **2 — Incident response and crisis coordination** | Containment, business decisions, regulatory reporting, customer protection and external coordination | Clear authority; independent communications; forensic readiness; bank/supplier playbooks; tested legal, treasury and operations involvement | Delayed containment, inconsistent decisions, missed notifications and unnecessary disruption |
| **2 / context-dependent — Facilities, OT and physical resilience** | Data-centre power/cooling, access control, branches, ATMs and cash operations | Inventory of relevant facilities controls; restricted remote maintenance; appropriate IT/OT separation; tested power and site contingencies | Loss of premises, cooling, cash access or hosting capacity compounds the cyber incident |



### Documented incidents: what they establish—and what they do not


**Configuration and governance failure:** Capital One’s 2019 incident involved a misconfigured web application firewall. The OCC’s 2020 action identified cloud-migration risk-assessment deficiencies and delayed correction. This supports secure configuration and effective risk governance; it does not establish that cloud use itself is inadequate. ([justice.gov](https://www.justice.gov/usao-wdwa/united-states-v-paige-thompson))([occ.gov](https://www.occ.gov/news-issuances/news-releases/2020/nr-occ-2020-101.html))



**Privileged compromise and containment:** River Financial’s June 2026 disclosure records ransomware, affected administrative accounts and systems taken offline. Those facts support the importance of privileged-access control and response readiness. They do **not** establish missing MFA, the initial exploit or destruction of backups. ([sec.gov](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1641601/000119312526282946/ck0001641601-20260619.htm))



**Supplier continuity:** the January 2023 ION incident interrupted derivatives processing, margin calculations and reporting. Workarounds and clearinghouse support prevented defaults; most services returned by 19 February. This demonstrates both the dependency and the value of coordinated alternatives, without proving that a particular preventive control was absent. ([home.treasury.gov](https://home.treasury.gov/system/files/261/FSOC2023AnnualReport.pdf))



**Payment integrity:** the Bangladesh Bank case demonstrates misuse of a bank’s local payment environment to generate fraudulent instructions. It supports independent transaction verification, segregation and anomaly detection—not a claim that SWIFT’s central network was breached. ([justice.gov](https://www.justice.gov/d9/press-releases/attachments/2018/09/06/2018_09_06_park_complaint_unsealed_0.pdf))



**Authentication is not sufficient by itself:** official Scattered Spider guidance documents MFA manipulation and account-recovery abuse. This is cross-sector evidence for testing the entire identity lifecycle, not evidence that every banking victim lacked MFA. ([cyber.gov.au](https://www.cyber.gov.au/advisory/scattered-spider))


## 3. Applicable standards and benchmarks

### Regulatory status and practical use


DORA has applied since **17 January 2025** and addresses ICT risk, incident handling, resilience testing and third-party technology risk for financial entities within its scope. Detailed obligations depend on entity classification and applicable technical standards; it should not be treated as a worldwide banking rule. [2](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2280055719552090160)




| Benchmark | Status and appropriate application | What it can establish |
|---|---|---|
| **EU DORA and applicable technical standards** | **Mandatory for in-scope entities**, with proportionality and differentiated testing obligations | Legal compliance baseline for ICT risk and resilience—not proof that a bank will withstand every scenario |
| **UK operational-resilience rules and PRA supervisory expectations** | **Rules are mandatory for in-scope firms; supervisory statements explain expectations.** Focus on important business services, impact tolerances, mapping and severe-but-plausible testing | Whether the firm can evidence service-level resilience, rather than only infrastructure availability |
| **US banking security requirements and FFIEC examination guidance** | Distinguish applicable laws/rules from **supervisory guidance**. Guidance does not become a new statute merely because examiners use it | Risk-based assessment of security, authentication, operations, continuity and outsourcing |
| **Basel operational-resilience principles and ICT risk practices** | **International supervisory principles/guidance**, implemented through jurisdictions rather than automatically binding every bank | A common basis for governance, dependencies, incident management and critical-operation resilience |
| **NIST CSF 2.0** | **Recommended, outcome-based framework** unless adopted through another obligation | Common language across Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond and Recover; useful for current/target assessment |
| **CRI Financial Services Cybersecurity Profile** | **Industry assessment framework**, not a substitute for legal analysis | Consolidated financial-sector control objectives and reuse of evidence across overlapping expectations |
| **ISO 27001 / ISO 22301** | **Voluntary management-system standards**, unless contractually or otherwise required | Scoped assurance over information-security or business-continuity management—not guaranteed service recovery |
| **SWIFT Customer Security Programme, payment-network and FMI safeguards** | **Participation- and service-dependent obligations or expectations** | Security of the relevant payment/messaging environment; not certification of the whole bank |




The Basel Committee’s **2 June 2026** primary publication identifies ICT risk management as a component of operational risk management supporting operational resilience. It is a range-of-practices report, not evidence that all jurisdictions mandate one technology architecture. [3](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2281500371530548405)



The CRI Profile is described as consolidating financial-sector regulatory expectations into diagnostic control objectives. The retrieved December 2023 industry account supports its use for harmonisation and evidence reuse; its adoption or a favourable assessment should not be presented as regulatory approval or a measured sector-average maturity score. [4](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2278567286908387344)



**Important distinctions**

- **Mandatory outcome versus implementation choice:** a requirement to control privileged access does not necessarily mandate a particular PAM product.
- **MFA versus effective identity protection:** enrolment, recovery, session handling, service identities and emergency access remain relevant.
- **Impact tolerance versus recovery objective:** a service-level tolerance is not interchangeable with an individual application’s recovery-time objective.
- **Backup versus recovery:** possession of copies does not establish clean, complete, timely restoration.
- **Certification versus operational assurance:** a certification’s scope and findings matter; it is not a universal guarantee.
- **No universal recovery time:** service criticality, settlement deadlines, customer harm and applicable obligations determine suitable objectives. FMI-specific expectations should not be applied indiscriminately to every bank application.


## 4. Empirical sector benchmarks and recurring preparedness weaknesses

### A. Supervisory testing: strongest evidence of operational capability


The ECB’s **26 July 2024** cyber-resilience stress-test findings covered **109 banks**, with enhanced testing at **28**. The exercise assumed a successful attack affecting core-system databases and assessed response and recovery. Banks had frameworks in place, but the ECB identified improvement needs, including business-continuity arrangements, recovery objectives, critical third-party dependencies and assessment of economic impact. This is exercise evidence—not a count of actual attacks. ([bankingsupervision.europa.eu](https://www.bankingsupervision.europa.eu/press/pr/date/2024/html/ssm.pr240726~06d5776a02.hu.html))



The ECB’s **December 2024 supervisory priorities** reported non-compliance in more than **10% of contracts covering critical functions** and highlighted provider, geographic and functional concentration. These findings support scrutiny of contractual and concentration management, but do not establish that each affected contract caused an incident. ([bankingsupervision.europa.eu](https://www.bankingsupervision.europa.eu/framework/priorities/pdf/ssm.supervisory_priorities202412~6f69ad032f.en.pdf))


### B. Industry research: useful, but not equivalent to audited maturity


Bitsight’s **November 2025** research examined observable connections involving **41,511 financial organisations**. Among its financial-sector customers, the average proportion of the observed supply chain monitored was **36.3%**, compared with **24.6%** for other organisations. The report itself notes uncertainty over whether unmonitored suppliers had been judged non-critical. These are vendor-observed monitoring measures across financial services—not a banking compliance rate or proof of inadequate monitoring at every firm. [5](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2280730163635307544)



The FCA’s **28 July 2026** publication reports that third-party issues accounted for **27% of incidents reported by firms in 2025**, with **37% of those third-party incidents cyber-related**. This indicates operational relevance, not the percentage of banks with weak third-party controls. ([fca.org.uk](https://www.fca.org.uk/news/blogs/strengthening-resilience-across-increasingly-interconnected-financial-system))



### Recurring gaps supported by the combined evidence

1. **Recovery plans are more complete than recovery evidence.** Technical restoration, transaction reconciliation and backlog clearance are not always assessed together.
2. **Supplier assurance can stop at contracts and questionnaires.** These do not demonstrate executable substitution or recovery.
3. **Identity protection can focus on login rather than the full lifecycle.** Recovery, privilege changes and third-party identities create additional pathways.
4. **Asset and service maps can diverge.** A system inventory may omit the dependencies required to deliver a payment or restore a ledger.
5. **Preparedness scores can obscure scope.** Self-assessment, external attack-surface ratings, penetration tests and service exercises measure different things.

There is **no defensible single worldwide “banking maturity average”** in the assembled evidence. Comparable benchmarking requires consistent populations, control definitions, assessment dates, testing depth and independent validation.


## 5. Subsector-specific safeguards and proportionality



| Business model | Additional emphasis |
|---|---|
| **Retail/digital banking** | Authentication and recovery, mobile/API security, account takeover, payment status, high-volume customer communications and channel resilience |
| **Community/regional banks and outsourced-core users** | Supplier-ledger access, joint recovery tests, retained internal decision capability and practical exit arrangements |
| **Investment banking/prime brokerage** | Position integrity, market connectivity, margin/collateral processing, manual capacity and reporting deadlines |
| **Custody/transaction banking** | Settlement instructions, securities/cash reconciliation, subcustodian/correspondent dependencies and corporate-action deadlines |
| **BaaS/sponsor-bank models** | Responsibility allocation, customer-level records, reconciliation, third-party access and access to funds during intermediary failure |
| **Mortgage/consumer/SME lenders** | Protection of underwriting documents and identity data, servicing continuity, collection/disbursement integrity and supplier retention |
| **ATM/cash-intensive operations** | Physical and software hardening, maintenance access, dispensing integrity, cash logistics and power/connectivity contingencies |

Facilities OT controls are appropriate where an institution owns, operates or materially depends on such systems. A bank does not need a manufacturing-style OT programme merely because it occupies an office. Similarly, multi-cloud, microsegmentation or an in-house SOC should be justified by risk and demonstrable outcomes—not presumed universally necessary.


## 6. Recommended improvement and assessment priorities


**1. Establish a service-level evidence baseline.** For each critical service, record ownership, dependencies, tolerances, recovery objectives, latest test, unresolved findings and residual risk.

**2. Test the full identity chain.** Include phishing-resistant methods where justified, helpdesk recovery, new-authenticator registration, privileged changes, supplier access and emergency administration.

**3. Prove clean recovery.** Restore essential data, configurations, keys and applications in a controlled environment; validate balances, positions, transaction status and backlog throughput.

**4. Exercise a critical-supplier failure.** Test loss of both service and supplier support, including alternative communications, retained records and realistic transition constraints.

**5. Prioritise exposure reduction by business impact.** Measure unknown assets, unsupported components and overdue high-risk remediation affecting critical services—not just total vulnerability counts.

**6. Demonstrate containment and detection.** Use exercises to establish whether identity, cloud, network and payment monitoring identifies abuse, and whether isolation preserves unaffected functions.

**7. Close findings through retesting.** Evidence of implementation is weaker than evidence that the original failure condition no longer succeeds.

### Suggested internal benchmarks—not asserted sector averages

- Percentage of critical services with validated dependency maps and current exercises.
- Coverage of privileged and third-party identities by appropriate authentication and access review.
- Age and business exposure of overdue high-risk vulnerabilities.
- Percentage of critical services restored successfully with integrity validation.
- Time to contain representative identity, supplier and ransomware scenarios.
- Tested fallback throughput versus the service’s minimum operating requirement.
- Critical suppliers with proven joint response/recovery arrangements.
- Repeat findings, overdue actions and successful remediation retests.

**Overall confidence:** high in the capability priorities and the cited supervisory findings; moderate in generalising recurring gaps across jurisdictions and business models. Institution-specific conclusions require scoped control evidence, not assumptions based on technology purchases or public incident publicity.
