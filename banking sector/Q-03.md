The investigation separates attack dates from disclosure dates, and direct bank compromises from supplier incidents and unverified claims. The register is evidence-backed but not exhaustive: public disclosures often omit initial access, complete recovery timelines and final financial costs.

# Banking cyber incidents: historical landmarks and recent developments
**Research cutoff: 27 October 2026**

- **12-month reporting window:** 28 October 2025–27 October 2026.
- **30-day reporting window:** 28 September–27 October 2026.
- **Register notation:** **U** = not established in the retrieved evidence—not zero, absent or disproved. Confidence applies to the stated finding; attribution is assessed separately.

## 1. Executive findings


Three distinct mechanisms dominate the consequential cases examined:

1. **Payment-integrity attacks:** legitimate banking infrastructure executes fraudulent instructions.
2. **Confidentiality breaches:** customer or institutional information is stolen without necessarily interrupting transaction processing.
3. **Availability and supply-chain attacks:** ransomware or supplier compromise impairs processing, reconciliation or reporting across organisations.

These mechanisms have different credit consequences. Stolen funds, remediation expenditure, litigation settlements, regulatory penalties and temporary settlement exposures should not be combined into a single “loss” figure.



Recent primary evidence demonstrates that continued banking availability does not mean an incident was immaterial: Standard Bank disclosed compromise of administrative/document systems while its core systems remained operational; SitusAMC’s customer notification confirmed acquisition of personal data associated with mortgage services; River Financial disclosed ransomware affecting operations. ([standardbank.co.za](https://www.standardbank.co.za/southafrica/news-and-media/newsroom/an-important-data-incident-update-14-april?utm_source=openai))



**Trend assessment:** the evidence supports recurring supplier exposure, credential/social-engineering attacks, ransomware and attacks against peripheral business systems. It does **not** support a statistically representative worldwide banking attack rate or a defensible percentage change for the exact 12-month window.

## 4. Last-12-month trend analysis

### Supplier exposure is recurrent, but its effects differ


Marquis, SitusAMC and DriveWealth demonstrate different forms of downstream data exposure. Their notices do not establish that each client bank’s own network was compromised. DriveWealth’s incident also involved records retained from earlier client relationships, demonstrating that ending an operational relationship does not necessarily eliminate retained-data exposure. ([assets.alm.com](https://assets.alm.com/b2/ac/08d910474aa0802db5c68c4b0cbf/marquis-letter-to-credit-unions-and-banks.pdf?utm_source=openai))



**Assessment:** supplier concentration can aggregate privacy, notification and litigation exposure even when payment services remain available. These should be distinguished from processing-supplier attacks such as ION, where continuity itself was impaired.


### Credential theft and social engineering extend beyond bank employees


Original research published **8 April 2026**, covering **2025 telemetry and observed underground data**, describes declining traditional PC banking-malware prevalence alongside growing mobile banking malware and infostealer-enabled credential theft. Its observations cover vendor-visible users and leaked credentials—not a census of bank-network intrusions. [7](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2281372897521958940)



Revolut’s fraudulent-government-request incident illustrates a separate mechanism: sensitive information can be disclosed through deception without an attacker exploiting a bank server vulnerability. ([cert.europa.eu](https://www.cert.europa.eu/publications/threat-intelligence/cb26-10?utm_source=openai))


### Peripheral systems remain consequential


Standard Bank identified administrative/document systems rather than core transaction systems as affected. South Korea’s regulator subsequently identified patching, unsupported-system management, monitoring and authentication deficiencies across its wider inspection population; those findings must not be assigned as the proven entry mechanism for every breached institution. ([standardbank.co.za](https://www.standardbank.co.za/southafrica/news-and-media/newsroom/an-important-data-incident-update-14-april?utm_source=openai))


### Ransomware combines operational disruption with uncertain data outcomes


River’s filings document both affected operations and data removal. Its later reliance on an attacker’s deletion representation does not establish that all copies were eliminated, and its July filing did not provide a completed financial-impact assessment. ([sec.gov](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1641601/000119312526282946/ck0001641601-20260619.htm?utm_source=openai))


### Physical–cyber attacks remain relevant


The FBI’s **February 2026** bulletin documents increasing US ATM jackpotting, including physical access and malware commands to cash-dispensing software. Its **2025** incident/loss figures support concern about that specific activity, not a worldwide increase in all banking attacks during the exact rolling window. ([fbi.gov](https://www.fbi.gov/file-repository/increase-in-malware-enabled-atm-jackpotting-incidents-across-united-states-021926.pdf?utm_source=openai))



**Observable change, with limits:** the evidence shows diversification across identity deception, retained supplier data, exposed support systems and ATM software. It does not establish that AI-enabled attacks have replaced conventional methods, or that highly publicised incidents represent typical banking losses.

## 6. Three detailed case studies

### Case study 1 — Bangladesh Bank: fraudulent instructions through trusted payment infrastructure


**Sequence and actor:** the DOJ complaint describes reconnaissance and employment-themed spear-phishing, whose links **may have provided initial access**. Attackers obtained access to bank terminals interfacing with SWIFT and generated apparently authentic payment instructions. US authorities attributed the operation to a North Korean conspiracy; the charging document remains an allegation, not a conviction. ([justice.gov](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/press-release/file/1092091/dl))



**Effects and financial accounting:** approximately **$81m** reached Philippine accounts and was laundered; a **$20m** Sri Lankan transfer was stopped. Those figures describe transaction outcomes, not a verified current net loss. A complete recovery duration and up-to-date recovery accounting are not established here. Treasury imposed sanctions on Park Jin Hyok and an associated entity in **September 2018**—action against alleged perpetrators, not a bank fine. ([justice.gov](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/press-release/file/1092091/dl))



**Resilience lesson:** authentication of a payment message does not establish the legitimacy of the underlying business instruction. Continuity and security require independent payment verification, anomalous-instruction detection and rapid cross-border escalation, not only protection of the messaging endpoint.


### Case study 2 — Capital One: cloud configuration, data theft and long-tail consequences


**Sequence:** the principal data access occurred on **22–23 March 2019**. A GitHub user alerted Capital One on **17 July**; the bank determined an intrusion on **19 July** and announced it on **29 July**. DOJ describes entry through a misconfigured web application firewall. The bank reported fixing the specific configuration vulnerability. ([investor.capitalone.com](https://investor.capitalone.com/static-files/1364d634-a3d5-464d-8505-ddf46eda928d?utm_source=openai))



**Actor and consequences:** Thompson was convicted in **June 2022**. The OCC’s **6 August 2020** action imposed an **$80m** penalty for deficiencies in cloud-migration risk assessment and delayed correction. DOJ also records the **$190m** customer settlement. These are separate legal/regulatory costs; neither is customer money stolen, and neither alone measures the complete incident cost. No precise bank-wide outage or full recovery duration is established. ([justice.gov](https://www.justice.gov/usao-wdwa/pr/former-seattle-tech-worker-convicted-wire-fraud-and-computer-intrusions?utm_source=openai))



**Resilience lesson:** cloud-provider use does not transfer responsibility for configuration, permissions and data protection. Operational availability can coexist with major confidentiality harm, remediation expenditure and multi-year legal consequences.


### Case study 3 — ION: ransomware propagated through market-processing dependencies


**Sequence and affected functions:** FSOC’s **2023 Annual Report** records a LockBit attack on **30 January 2023** affecting XTP. Of approximately 60 CFTC-registered futures commission merchants, **16 used XTP**, with varying effects. Trade feeds, matching, allocation, clearing, margin calculations and reporting required workarounds. Initial access and exploited vulnerability remain U. ([home.treasury.gov](https://home.treasury.gov/system/files/261/FSOC2023AnnualReport.pdf))



**Recovery and consequences:** most services were restored by **19 February**. Manual processing and assistance from clearinghouses supported timely margin obligations; **no defaults occurred**. The CFTC delayed its Commitments of Traders report because affected firms could not supply timely, accurate data. Aggregate monetary loss, verified ransom and an incident-specific regulatory penalty are U. ([home.treasury.gov](https://home.treasury.gov/system/files/261/FSOC2023AnnualReport.pdf))



**Resilience lesson:** an intact bank network is insufficient when a critical processing supplier is unavailable. Effective alternatives must preserve positions, margin, reconciliation and reporting—not merely reconnect systems. ION also demonstrates that substantial disruption need not become a participant default when workarounds and coordinated response function.


## 7. Exclusions, unresolved facts and credit-intelligence implications


**Do not classify all banking outages or data exposures as attacks.** UK parliamentary evidence attributes Lloyds Banking Group’s **12 March 2026** data-display incident to a software defect during an overnight update. It is excluded from the cyberattack register, despite its privacy and compensation consequences. ([committees.parliament.uk](https://committees.parliament.uk/committee/158/treasury-committee/news/212885/nearly-half-a-million-lloyds-banking-group-customers-affected-by-personal-data-glitch/?utm_source=openai))



The **October 2026 Google Cloud disruption** is likewise excluded from attack counts: the retrieved reporting establishes service disruption, not a confirmed cyberattack. [9](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2281826677963096064)



### Material evidence gaps

- **Initial access and exploited weaknesses:** unknown for most recent cases; no CVE is inferred from a supplier’s product name.
- **Attribution:** criminal charges, government attribution, vendor assessments and ransomware claims have different evidential status.
- **Recovery:** containment, service resumption, backlog clearance and full remediation are different milestones.
- **Financial effects:** final net losses, insurance recoveries and legal costs are often unavailable or reported on incompatible bases.
- **Geographic coverage:** recent detail is strongest for the US, South Korea, South Africa, India and selected cross-border cases. This is not evidence of lower activity elsewhere.
- **Deduplication:** supplier notices, downstream customer notifications, revised victim counts and enforcement actions are not additional attacks. Related Korean incidents remain separate victim entries because a common actor/campaign was not established.
- **Statistical representativeness:** the register is selected for significance and evidential support, not sampled to estimate incident frequency or average banking loss.

**Credit conclusion:** operational interruption and confidentiality harm must be assessed separately. The most decision-useful issuer disclosures identify affected business functions, trustworthy-data recovery, backlog clearance, supplier exposure and realised costs. Where those facts remain unknown, a precise financial-loss estimate or confident resilience judgment is not justified.
