# Banking: cyber financial impact, disruption and credit transmission
**Research cutoff: 27 October 2026 | Worldwide scope**

## 1. Principal conclusions


1. **No defensible banking-only worldwide average or median incident cost emerges from the evidence.** The strongest cost studies cover broader financial services, often including insurance and investment companies.
2. **Breach lifecycle, service interruption and full recovery are different measures.** Months spent detecting a breach do not imply months without banking services.
3. **Liquidity stress can exceed the recognised accounting loss.** Settlement demands, deposit withdrawals or collateral requirements can create immediate funding needs without constituting permanent losses.
4. **Credit effects depend on the business model.** Lost origination and servicing revenue can be especially important for specialist lenders; settlement and intraday liquidity matter for transaction banks; deposit confidence and replacement funding matter for deposit-taking banks.
5. **Benchmark costs should not be scaled mechanically by records, assets or outage weeks.** Borrower-specific service, funding and recovery data are needed.


## 2. Financial metrics and methodological caveats

### A. Mean breach cost: five-year financial-services series


IBM’s financial-industry figures cover **banking, insurance and investment companies**, not banking alone. Its published financial-industry analysis provides the 2021–24 series; the original 2025 report supplies the subsequent observation. Values below are nominal **US dollars per studied breach**, not annual expected losses. [1](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2279413842058413618) [2](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2280503664239983616)

| Report year | Financial-industry mean breach cost | Coverage and evidential qualification |
|---|---:|---|
| **2021** | **US$5.72m** | Multinational financial-industry benchmark; banking-only sample and median unavailable |
| **2022** | **US$5.97m** | Same broad industry concept; different annual sample |
| **2023** | **US$5.90m** | Not a longitudinal observation of the same firms |
| **2024** | **US$6.08m** | Financial industry, not all-sector global mean |
| **2025** | **US$5.56m** | Original report; total sample **600 organisations**, financial industry **14%**, a rounded share rather than an exact disclosed sector count |

The 2025 study covered breaches occurring **March 2024–February 2025**, across **17 industries and 16 countries/geographic regions**, with **3,470 interviews**. It used activity-based costing, including direct expenditure, indirect costs and estimated lost business. Studied breaches involved **2,960–113,620 records**. Its sampling was nonstatistical, changed annually and did not support conventional confidence intervals. [2](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2280503664239983616)



**Calculated trend:** the reported financial-industry mean increased approximately **6.3% between 2021 and 2024**, then fell **8.6% in 2025**, ending approximately **2.8% below 2021**. This is a nominal benchmark series, not an inflation-adjusted banking loss index. Sample composition and currency conversion can affect changes.



**Latest 2026 indication—lower evidential weight:** secondary coverage reports a financial-services mean of **US$6.29m**. The retrieved reporting describes an overall study of **602 organisations worldwide**, but does not establish the financial-services subsample or a sector median. The primary 2026 industry table was not available for reconciliation. This value should remain separate from the better-supported five-year series; some 2026 coverage also gives a prior-year figure inconsistent with the original 2025 report’s **US$5.56m**. [3](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2281821390271152279) [4](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2281673544611931968) [2](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2280503664239983616)

**Additional gaps:** no defensible worldwide banking-specific mean or median was established for service interruption duration, operational recovery time, recovery/remediation expenditure, revenue loss or profit reduction. Available studies and cases use different definitions, populations and reporting endpoints. “Not established” does not mean zero or that no proprietary dataset exists.


### C. Cross-sector context—not banking benchmarks


IBM’s **2025 all-industry** study reported:

- **86%** of the 600 breached organisations experienced operational disruption.
- Mean identification/containment lifecycle of **241 days**.
- **65%** were still recovering at interview.
- Among organisations reporting full recovery, **76%** took more than 100 days.

Its definition of full recovery included normal operations, compliance obligations, restored confidence and preventive improvements. It is therefore broader than technical restoration or customer-service downtime. These figures must **not** be relabelled as banking averages. [2](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2280503664239983616)


## 3. Interpreting disruption and five-year trends



| Measure | Start and endpoint | Financial relevance |
|---|---|---|
| **Breach identification** | Compromise to discovery | Potential duration of hidden exposure; not necessarily lost service |
| **Containment** | Discovery to stopping/limiting the incident | Emergency response and isolation costs; services may be restricted |
| **Service interruption** | Loss/degradation to restoration of a specified service | Fees, customer harm, settlement and processing consequences |
| **Operational recovery** | Restoration plus validation, reconciliation and backlog clearance | Overtime, duplicate/manual processing, errors and delayed cash flows |
| **Full business recovery** | Operations plus legal, regulatory, customer and organisational resolution | Long-tail costs extending beyond the initial outage |

The supported trend is **not a simple continuous escalation**: financial-industry mean breach costs rose through 2024 before falling in 2025; reported median ransom payments rose sharply between the 2023 and 2024 Sophos samples. Different samples and missing payment denominators prevent treating that payment change as a stable trend for all banks.


## 4. Documented impact case studies

### Capital One: long-tail legal and regulatory cost without a demonstrated prolonged outage


The 2019 breach led to an **US$80m OCC penalty in 2020** and an **US$190m customer settlement** subsequently documented by DOJ. They are distinct legal/regulatory consequences—not stolen customer funds or a complete incident-cost total. A precise attributable reduction in revenue, funding capacity, covenant headroom or credit rating is not established here. ([occ.gov](https://www.occ.gov/news-issuances/news-releases/2020/nr-occ-2020-101.html))([justice.gov](https://www.justice.gov/usao-wdwa/pr/former-seattle-tech-worker-convicted-wire-fraud-and-computer-intrusions))



**Transmission:** legal charges and remediation can reduce retained earnings and capital generation after technical containment. Cash payments may occur in different periods from accounting recognition. Adding all announced estimates, provisions, settlements and payments would risk double-counting.


### loanDepot: origination and servicing interruption with disclosed expenditure


The January 2024 incident disrupted systems and access to the online loan-payment portal. By August, reporting described nearly **US$27m of ransomware-related costs**, including approximately **US$25m related to class-action litigation**. The retrieved account does not reconcile gross versus net insurance treatment, final full-year costs or attributable lost originations and revenue. loanDepot is a **nonbank mortgage-lending comparator**, not a deposit-taking bank. [7](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2278910478333771941) [8](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2279406642619482255)



**Transmission:** specialist lenders can lose origination fees and gain-on-sale income, incur servicing costs and retain loans longer on warehouse funding. These are plausible pathways; their amounts must not be attributed to loanDepot without financial-disclosure reconciliation.


### ION: processing disruption without participant default


FSOC’s account of the **30 January 2023** ION attack records effects on trade processing, margin and reporting, with most services restored by **19 February**. Manual processing and clearinghouse support enabled margin obligations to be met; **no defaults occurred**. No comparable aggregate loss or revenue-impact figure is established. This is a supplier case affecting banking and markets operations. ([home.treasury.gov](https://home.treasury.gov/system/files/261/FSOC2023AnnualReport.pdf))



**Transmission:** operational substitution can prevent a liquidity event even when normal processing remains unavailable. Costs may still arise through manual work, constrained capacity and reporting remediation. Roughly three weeks to majority restoration is an incident milestone, not a sector recovery average.


### ICBC Financial Services: liquidity exposure is not the same as permanent loss


November 2023 reporting describes a temporary **US$9bn obligation to BNY Mellon** following the ransomware-related processing disruption. It also reports LockBit’s claim of ransom payment, which was not independently verified in that account. The US$9bn must not be presented as an incident expense or net theft. [9](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2278501787499495597)



**Transmission:** a settlement interruption can create a funding requirement much larger than forensic expenditure. Materiality depends on duration, collateral, enforceable support, access to cash and whether obligations can be completed—not merely the nominal exposure.


## 5. Credit transmission analysis



| Channel | Banking-specific financial transmission | What should be assessed |
|---|---|---|
| **Revenue and margins** | Lost payment/trading fees, waived charges, customer attrition, missed originations; higher funding costs reduce net interest income | Permanently lost versus deferred activity; recurring income versus one-off charges |
| **Expenses and investment** | Forensics, legal support, customer protection, overtime, rebuilding and accelerated security programmes | Gross expense, insurance reimbursement, capitalised investment and ongoing operating-cost increases |
| **Liquidity and borrowing** | Deposit withdrawals, settlement obligations, margin/collateral demands and replacement wholesale funding | Accessible liquidity by entity/currency; collateral eligibility; borrowing capacity and operational ability to draw |
| **Capital** | Recognised losses reduce retained earnings; risk or supervisory responses may add pressure | CET1/leverage buffers, tax treatment, RWA changes and distribution restrictions |
| **Working capital/cash conversion** | Less useful as industrial metrics for banks; relevant equivalents are settlement timing, receivables, trapped collateral and payment queues | Avoid treating payment notional as revenue or a settlement delay as a permanent loss |
| **Specialist-lender cash conversion** | Delayed loan sales or securitisations extend warehouse usage; interrupted collections delay cash receipts | Advance rates, eligibility, margin calls, maturity concentration and liquidity covenants |
| **Debt service** | Lower earnings and higher funding costs reduce capacity; cash may be trapped in an operating subsidiary | Bank versus holding-company resources, upstreaming restrictions and contractual payment dates |
| **Ratings and covenants** | Repeated failures or persistent franchise/control damage can affect assessments and funding access | Actual rating actions and contractual definitions; no automatic downgrade or covenant breach should be assumed |

**EBITDA caution:** for deposit-taking banks, net interest income, pre-provision operating profit, cost-to-income, credit losses, liquidity and regulatory capital are generally more decision-useful than industrial EBITDA or net-debt/EBITDA. Debt covenants and coverage measures still matter at holding companies, nonbank lenders and suppliers where contractually applicable.

No cyber-attributable covenant breach, debt-service default or specific rating downgrade is established for the cases above. That is an evidence limitation—not assurance that credit transmission cannot occur.


## 6. Illustrative one-, four- and eight-week disruption scenarios


### Common assumptions

These are **hypothetical sensitivities, not historical averages or probability forecasts**.

Consider a deposit-taking bank with **US$1bn annual operating income**, including **US$365m fee income**; annual pre-tax profit **US$200m**. Assume:

- An affected service contributes **US$1m of normal fee income per day**.
- Alternative operations preserve **50%** of affected activity.
- Existing loan interest largely continues to accrue; total revenue is **not** assumed to stop.
- Incremental replacement funding is **US$500m**, at a **2 percentage-point annual spread**, using a 360-day basis.
- No ransom, fraud loss, fine, insurance receipt or tax benefit is included.
- Costs below are incremental and exclude the fee/funding effects already shown.

| Assumption / consequence | **One week: 7 days** | **Four weeks: 28 days** | **Eight weeks: 56 days** |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Operational state | Digital/payment service impaired; alternatives work | Prolonged core/supplier impairment; manual queues grow | Extended disruption and integrity validation; franchise pressure |
| Share of unprocessed fee activity permanently lost | 40% | 60% | 75% |
| **Illustrative lost fees** | **US$1.4m** | **US$8.4m** | **US$21.0m** |
| Response, remediation and customer-support expense | US$5.0m | US$15.0m | US$30.0m |
| Incremental funding cost | US$0.19m | US$0.78m | US$1.56m |
| **Illustrative pre-tax profit reduction** | **US$6.59m** | **US$24.18m** | **US$52.56m** |
| Reduction versus assumed annual pre-tax profit | 3.3% | 12.1% | 26.3% |

**Calculation:** lost fees = days × US$1m × 50% unavailable activity × permanently lost share. Funding cost = US$500m × 2% × days/360.

The US$500m borrowing requirement is **not** itself a profit loss. Likewise, the model does not imply a US$500m deposit outflow or that all delayed fees disappear. Long-tail legal costs, customer attrition and post-restoration backlogs could extend beyond each scenario.


### Business-model adaptations and offsets


- **Custody/transaction bank:** replace generic fee assumptions with failed settlement throughput, intraday liquidity, overdrafts and penalty/compensation exposure.
- **Retail/digital bank:** model deposit behaviour, payment/card usage, fee waivers and customer-support capacity; branches help only if their back ends function.
- **Investment bank:** model restricted trading capacity, hedge execution, collateral mobility and counterparty limits—not simply daily revenue multiplied by outage days.
- **Mortgage/specialist lender:** model lost versus delayed originations, loan-sale timing, warehouse interest and covenant/eligibility headroom.
- **BaaS model:** test access to customer-level records and funds separately from the availability of the fintech interface.

**Possible offsets:** functioning alternative rails, manual operations, clean recoverable data, existing earnings, immediately drawable liquidity, enforceable group support and insurance.

**Insurance limitations:** coverage, deductibles, waiting periods, sublimits, exclusions, aggregation and claims timing matter. A possible future recovery should not be netted against immediate cash needs without support.


## 7. Borrower-specific information required


A materiality assessment needs:

1. Revenue and contribution by affected service; transaction volumes, deadlines and substitution capacity.
2. Recovery milestones, integrity checks and backlog-clearance throughput—not merely a target restart date.
3. Cash expenditure and accounting provisions, reconciled to avoid double-counting; separate capital expenditure and insurance.
4. Deposit concentration, stressed outflows, available collateral, currency/entity liquidity and executable borrowing facilities.
5. Baseline profitability, capital buffers, debt maturities and restrictions on holding-company cash upstreaming.
6. Relevant covenants, warehouse eligibility, securitisation triggers and customer compensation obligations.
7. Actual rating-agency commentary, supervisory measures and customer attrition evidence.

**Confidence:** high in the original 2025 cost methodology and documented primary case consequences; moderate in extrapolating broader financial-services benchmarks to banking; lower in the unreconciled 2026 industry estimate and secondary case-cost reporting. Confidence in an individual borrower’s financial outcome remains limited without the inputs above.
