# Banking: inherent ICT exposure and cybersecurity maturity
**Evidence cutoff: 27 October 2026 | Scope: worldwide, with EU, UK and US emphasis**

## 1. Overall assessment


**Inherent Cyber/ICT exposure: high.** Banking depends on continuously available, interconnected systems and trustworthy transaction records. A disruption can prevent payments, settlement, collateral movements or access to funds even without a data breach.

**Observed maturity: uneven, with evidence of established capabilities and material gaps.** Supervisory testing and industry research support neither a blanket “highly mature” assessment nor a conclusion that banking is generally poorly defended.

**Residual risk: borrower-specific and undetermined at sector level.** It depends on the institution’s critical services, architecture, suppliers, tested controls and financial capacity. Regulatory intensity, cybersecurity expenditure and possession of security tools are not substitutes for demonstrated recovery.


## 2. Structural exposure indicators

### Technology intensity by subsector


The following is an operating-model assessment—not a measured ranking of individual institutions.

| Subsector | Digital dependency and critical systems | Principal concentration or continuity exposure |
|---|---|---|
| **Retail/commercial banking** | Core ledger, identity, cards, payments, loan servicing, branches and customer channels | Several channels can depend on the same ledger, authentication or processor |
| **Digital-only banks** | Apps/APIs, digital onboarding, cloud services, fraud controls and automated support | Little customer-facing substitution if digital access fails; cloud-native does not mean independently recoverable |
| **Community/regional banks** | Often externally provided core processing, digital banking and payments | Bundled providers and limited migration capacity can constrain alternatives |
| **Universal banks** | Retail plus markets, custody, treasury and cross-border infrastructure | Group-wide identity, software and shared operations can link otherwise diversified businesses |
| **Investment banking/prime brokerage** | Trading, market data, positions, risk, clearing, margin and collateral | Deadline-sensitive processing; manual alternatives may not sustain normal throughput |
| **Custody/transaction banking** | Cash/securities records, settlement instructions, correspondents and subcustodians | Accurate records and external settlement access are both necessary |
| **BaaS/sponsor-bank models** | Fintech interfaces, middleware, customer-level records and bank payment access | Fragmented responsibility and reconciliation across several organisations |
| **Specialist lenders** | Origination, credit data, document stores, servicing and funding interfaces | Servicing can continue while origination fails—or vice versa; delayed loan sales can increase funding needs |



### Cloud, legacy, remote connectivity and IT/OT


Bitsight’s **November 2025** original research analysed **41,511 financial organisations** and identified **50,232 third-party technology-provider relationships** through observable technical connections. It identified broad technology dependencies alongside finance-specialist providers, including Bloomberg, Murex, FactSet, Fiserv and identity/security suppliers. It also identified COBOL support and building-automation/access-control dependencies. This is broader financial-sector evidence, not a complete inventory of every bank or proof of each supplier’s criticality. [1](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2280730163635307544)



**Exposure implications:**

- **Cloud and managed services:** concentrate hosting, administration and recovery dependencies; different direct suppliers can share an underlying provider.
- **Legacy systems:** can create specialist-support and integration constraints. Age or use of a mainframe does not itself establish vulnerability.
- **Remote connectivity:** supplier access, employee access and management tools extend the trust boundary beyond bank premises.
- **IT/OT convergence:** relevant mainly to data-centre power/cooling, building access, ATMs and physical-service infrastructure. Banking should not be treated as a manufacturing sector with universal industrial-control exposure.
- **Recovery concentration:** spare sites may share identity, keys, software and administrators with production.

No reliable worldwide banking percentage was established for cloud-hosted critical workloads, unsupported systems, exposed remote services or independently recoverable environments.


## 3. Critical attack surface and vulnerability exposure



| Attack surface | Exposure mechanism | Important evidential distinction |
|---|---|---|
| **Customer apps, web services and APIs** | Public interfaces, authentication and integration errors; denial of service | An available login page does not demonstrate availability of settlement or reconciliation |
| **Identity and privileged access** | Credential theft, manipulated resets, excessive permissions and service-account abuse | MFA presence differs from effective enrolment, recovery and session protection |
| **Remote-access and management infrastructure** | Exploitation or misuse of legitimate administration paths | A vulnerable product used in finance is not proof of exploitation at a bank |
| **Cloud/SaaS and data stores** | Misconfiguration, excessive permissions, stolen sessions/tokens and bulk extraction | Customer configuration failure differs from provider-platform compromise |
| **Software and supplier connections** | Vulnerable components, compromised integrations or supplier access | Downstream exposure can occur without intrusion into the bank’s own network |
| **Payment, ledger and recovery infrastructure** | High-value access and difficult restoration dependencies | Integrity failure can be more consequential than loss of a customer channel |




**Common vulnerability pressure—cross-sector, not a banking prevalence estimate:** Verizon’s **2026 DBIR** reports vulnerability exploitation as the initial access vector in **31%** of its relevant breach dataset. Its separate vulnerability dataset found **26%** of observed CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities fully remediated and a **43-day median** to full remediation. These populations and denominators differ; none is a banking-specific patching score. [2](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2281466836425914728)



No specific CVE should be assigned to a bank without evidence connecting exploitation to that institution. Likewise, the incidents previously reviewed do not establish sector-wide absence of MFA, backup destruction or ineffective segmentation.


## 4. Sector maturity and preparedness evidence

| Evidence | What it establishes | Limitations / confidence |
|---|---|---|
| **ECB cyber-resilience exercise, July 2024** | Tested response/recovery at **109 banks**, with enhanced testing at **28**; identified improvement needs despite existing frameworks. [3](https://www.bankingsupervision.europa.eu/press/pr/date/2024/html/ssm.pr240726~06d5776a02.hu.html) | Strong preparedness evidence within the tested population. A simulated successful attack is not an observed breach or a test of prevention. **High confidence.** |
| **BIS working paper, 26 May 2026** | Using confidential ECB supervisory data, researchers identify European banks underinvesting relative to their cyber-risk profiles and examine supervisory incentives. [4](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2281483054222410654) | Supports heterogeneity, not a worldwide underinvestment rate. A research paper is not itself a binding supervisory finding. **High confidence in the stated research scope.** |
| **ECB supervisory priorities, December 2024** | Reported non-compliance in more than **10% of contracts covering critical functions** and highlighted concentration concerns. [5](https://www.bankingsupervision.europa.eu/framework/priorities/pdf/ssm.supervisory_priorities202412~6f69ad032f.en.pdf) | Contract deficiencies are not proof that each arrangement caused an incident. **High confidence within scope.** |
| **Bitsight supply-chain research, November 2025** | Financial-sector suppliers performed slightly worse than customers across **16 of 22** observed risk vectors, with differences up to **15%**. [1](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2280730163635307544) | External observations are not internal audits. Supplier footprint and compensating controls can affect interpretation. **Moderate confidence for maturity inference.** |
| **UK third-party incident reporting, July 2026** | FCA reports that **27%** of firms’ reported 2025 incidents involved third-party issues; **37% of those** were cyber-related. ### Detection, containment and recovery are not interchangeable


**Preparedness is strongest when evidenced through completed business outcomes:**

- **Detection:** useful coverage of identities, cloud, endpoints, suppliers and payments—not simply alert volume.
- **Containment:** isolating compromised access without unnecessarily disabling unaffected services.
- **Recovery:** restoring clean systems and establishing correct balances, positions and transaction status.
- **Continuity:** sustaining minimum service and clearing backlogs within business tolerances.

The assembled evidence does not provide a comparable worldwide banking distribution for detection time, containment time, clean-restoration success or tested fallback throughput. Public incident disclosures establish individual actions and outcomes, not sector-wide maturity.


## 5. Comparative benchmarks and incident trends

### Common-dataset comparisons


**Supply-chain monitoring—Bitsight, 2025:** nearly **600 financial-sector customers** collectively monitored over **46,000 organisations**. They monitored an average **36.3%** of their inferred supply chains, compared with **24.6%** for other organisations in the same research.

This indicates greater observed monitoring coverage—not superior overall resilience. The report explicitly notes that some unmonitored suppliers may have been judged non-critical, and monitoring may occur outside Bitsight. [1](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2280730163635307544)



**Ransomware—Sophos 2024 survey, reported July 2024:** the common survey covered **5,000 IT/cybersecurity leaders**, including **592 financial-services respondents**, across **14 countries**, in organisations with **100–5,000 employees**. Fieldwork was January–February 2024 and concerned the preceding year.

- **65%** of financial-services respondents reported ransomware, versus **59%** across sectors.
- Among victim organisations, the mean proportion of computers affected was **43%** in financial services versus **49%** overall; the reported figures were **62%** for energy/oil/gas/utilities and **58%** for healthcare.

These comparisons come from secondary reporting of the survey, cover more than banking and do not control for organisational composition. They show that reported victimisation and impact breadth can move differently. [7](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2279368171842437123)


### Latest broad incident direction


Verizon’s **2026 DBIR**, published **19 May**, analysed more than **31,000 incidents** and **22,000 confirmed breaches** across **145 countries**. Its methodology specifies incidents from **1 November 2024–31 October 2025**.

Across the overall dataset, ransomware appeared in **48% of breaches**, compared with **44%** in the preceding report; third-party involvement reached **48%**, compared with **30%**. The report also describes expanded contributors and collection of large public campaigns. Its financial category includes **insurance**, and the retrieved sector pages were insufficient for a validated bank-specific comparison. [2](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2281466836425914728)



**Interpretation:** exploitation, ransomware and supplier-related breaches remain important pressures. Their shares in a contributed dataset are not annual probabilities, population incidence rates or proof that banking maturity deteriorated.

No reliable common dataset established a comprehensive global ranking of banking against healthcare, manufacturing, retail and utilities on actual end-to-end resilience.


## 6. Reporting bias and significant gaps


Comparisons require adjustment in interpretation—not invented numerical corrections:

- **Detection bias:** better-monitored institutions may identify more incidents.
- **Disclosure bias:** regulated banks can report more than less-regulated businesses.
- **Contributor bias:** forensic, insurance and vendor datasets reflect their customer and case mix.
- **Definition bias:** attempted attacks, availability incidents, confirmed data breaches and customer fraud differ.
- **Scope bias:** financial services/insurance is not banking; smaller institutions and many jurisdictions are underrepresented.
- **Time bias:** report publication year differs from the observation period.
- **Measurement bias:** external ratings, self-reported controls, supervisory exercises and audited service tests measure different dimensions.

### Priority evidence gaps

1. Bank-only, subsector- and size-adjusted incident rates with meaningful exposure denominators.
2. Critical-workload cloud concentration and underlying fourth-party dependencies.
3. Supported versus unsupported legacy assets and actual internet exposure.
4. Tested restoration integrity, recovery independence and backlog capacity.
5. Comparable internal-audit or supervisory control findings across EU, UK and US populations.
6. Greater coverage of emerging-market banking systems.

**Final judgement:** high inherent technological exposure is well supported. There is credible evidence of established monitoring and recovery frameworks, alongside investment, supplier and recovery gaps. **A uniform sector maturity grade is not defensible.** For credit analysis, the differentiator is borrower-specific evidence that essential transactions, trustworthy records and liquidity access survive a realistic disruption.
