# Banking sector cyber threat assessment
**As of 27 October 2026 | Worldwide, with EU, UK and US analysis**

## 1. Prioritised threat scenarios


**Overall assessment:** the most material threats combine trusted access with operational concentration. A valid identity, privileged administrator account, payment instruction or supplier connection can carry an attack past perimeter controls.

The priorities below reflect **potential operational severity, breadth of exposure and strength of evidence—not estimated attack probabilities**. Scenarios overlap: identity compromise can lead to ransomware, and supplier compromise can cause either data theft or service interruption.

**Severity:** *Critical* means potential interruption of essential banking or market functions; *High* means material business-line disruption, fraud or confidentiality harm. Ratings describe plausible consequences, not inevitable outcomes.




| Priority | Threat scenario | Particularly exposed subsectors | Operational severity | Direction and assessment confidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Privileged compromise leading to ransomware, destructive effects or untrusted data** | All banks; universal banks and institutions with shared group infrastructure | **Critical** | Persistent threat; faster execution and infrastructure-level targeting increase concern. **High** confidence in ransomware pathway; **moderate** in banking-specific destructive tail risk |
| **2** | **Critical supplier compromise causing multi-institution interruption or data exposure** | Community/regional banks, BaaS, transaction banking, custody, derivatives businesses | **Critical** for processing interruption; **High** for data exposure | Recurrent exposure; exact worldwide growth rate unestablished. **High** confidence |
| **3** | **Identity and business-process deception enabling cloud/SaaS or customer-data theft** | Digital banks, consumer lenders, wealth/private banking, universal banks | **High**, potentially critical if privileged access expands | Increasing automation and adaptable impersonation; not every campaign is growing. **High** confidence in mechanisms; **moderate** in prevalence |
| **4** | **Payment manipulation, account takeover and malware-enabled cash theft** | Retail/commercial banks, transaction banks, central banks, ATM operators | **High–Critical** | Continuing diversification; documented US ATM increase, but no defensible global banking frequency estimate. **High** confidence |
| **5** | **DDoS and coordinated disruption of customer access, APIs and shared infrastructure** | Digital banks, payments and transaction banking; externally connected markets businesses | **High**, potentially critical when prolonged or combined with another failure | Increased persistence in observed financial-services DDoS. **High** confidence in observed activity; **moderate** in systemic consequences |



## 2. Scenario and attack-pathway analysis

### 1 — Privileged compromise → ransomware or loss of trustworthy operations


**Observed banking evidence:** River Financial’s disclosure identifies unauthorised access around **16 June 2026**, detection around **19 June**, and ransomware across portions of its server environment. Containment included disabling affected administrative accounts and taking systems offline. Subsequent disclosure confirmed data removal. **Initial access, exploited vulnerability, missing MFA and backup destruction were not established.** ([sec.gov](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1641601/000119312526282946/ck0001641601-20260619.htm))



**Actor and TTP relevance:** the **29 July 2025** multinational Scattered Spider advisory documents helpdesk/social-engineering attacks, MFA manipulation, remote-access tools, discovery of credentials and infrastructure, data theft and ransomware. It includes reported DragonForce deployment against ESXi. These are documented cross-sector techniques; they must not be assigned to River or another bank without incident-specific evidence. ([cyber.gov.au](https://www.cyber.gov.au/advisory/scattered-spider?utm_source=openai))



**Plausible pathway:** stolen credentials, manipulated account recovery or exploitation of an exposed service → privileged access → discovery of directories, virtualisation, storage and backup systems → exfiltration and encryption → isolation of systems and prolonged reconciliation.

**Enabling/amplifying weaknesses:** excessive administrator rights; reusable credentials; production and recovery sharing an administrative boundary; poorly monitored hypervisors; exposed remote management; insufficient segmentation; untested restoration.

**Operational consequences:** unavailable accounts or servicing, delayed payment processing, uncertain positions, manual backlogs and inability to demonstrate correct balances. Deliberate ledger corruption is a severe plausible extension—not a confirmed outcome of the River incident.

**Actor motivation:** extortion and data monetisation are central to this scenario. A destructive state operation could produce similar operational effects, but its attribution and objectives require separate evidence.



**EU resilience evidence:** the ECB’s **2024** exercise tested **109 banks**, including enhanced testing at **28**, against a fictitious attack severely affecting core-system databases. It found response/recovery frameworks in place but areas for improvement. This supports the materiality of recovery risk—not a claim that those banks suffered actual destructive attacks. ([bankingsupervision.europa.eu](https://www.bankingsupervision.europa.eu/press/pr/date/2024/html/ssm.pr240726~06d5776a02.hu.html?utm_source=openai))


### 2 — Supplier compromise → correlated banking interruption or exposure


**Observed market interruption:** FSOC records that the **30 January 2023 LockBit attack on ION** disabled XTP services used by **16 of approximately 60 CFTC-registered futures commission merchants**. Most services returned by **19 February**. Manual processing and clearinghouse assistance enabled margin obligations to be met; no defaults occurred. Initial access was not established in that account. ([home.treasury.gov](https://home.treasury.gov/system/files/261/FSOC2023AnnualReport.pdf))



**Observed downstream exposure:** a Marquis notification records unauthorised access associated with the **August 2025** incident and possible copying of files. It explicitly says the incident was limited to Marquis’s systems and did not affect its customer’s systems. Supplier data exposure is therefore not equivalent to compromise of each bank’s network. ([mass.gov](https://www.mass.gov/doc/2026-408-marquis-software-solutions/download?utm_source=openai))



**Plausible pathways:**
- Supplier intrusion → hosted processing unavailable → multiple banks lose the same function.
- Supplier intrusion → retained customer data stolen → multiple institutions face notifications and fraud exposure.
- Compromised remote administration or integration → access extends into customers. This third pathway is plausible but is **not established by the Marquis notice**.

**Enabling/amplifying weaknesses:** concentrated services; persistent supplier privileges; opaque subcontracting; weak tenant separation; inadequate access revocation; excessive data retention; untested exit arrangements; dependence on the supplier for incident diagnosis and recovery.

**Consequences:** clearing or settlement backlogs, interrupted digital banking, delayed reporting and correlated confidentiality harm. Smaller banks may have fewer practical alternatives; large banks can still depend on specialised infrastructure that cannot be replaced quickly.

**Confidence:** high in supplier-transmitted risk; lower in any claim of universal fourth-party access or inevitable contagion.



**Supervisory evidence:** the ECB’s **2025–27 priorities**, published in **December 2024**, reported non-compliance in more than **10% of contracts covering critical functions** and highlighted provider, geographic and functional concentration. Contractual deficiencies are supervisory findings—not proof of successful exploitation. ([bankingsupervision.europa.eu](https://www.bankingsupervision.europa.eu/framework/priorities/pdf/ssm.supervisory_priorities202412~6f69ad032f.en.pdf?utm_source=openai))


### 3 — Identity or process deception → data theft and extortion


**Observed process failure:** CERT-EU’s **September 2026** summary records Revolut’s **12 September** disclosure that government-agency impersonation by email induced release of customer information, including identity documents and account/transaction records. This demonstrates deceptive data requests; it does **not** demonstrate a technical intrusion into Revolut’s core platform or absence of MFA. ([cert.europa.eu](https://www.cert.europa.eu/publications/threat-intelligence/cb26-10?utm_source=openai))



**Historical technical failure:** DOJ documents entry into Capital One’s stored data through a **misconfigured web application firewall in 2019**. The OCC subsequently identified deficiencies in cloud-migration risk assessment and delayed correction. This is evidence of customer-side configuration/governance failure, not a general compromise of the cloud provider. ([justice.gov](https://www.justice.gov/usao-wdwa/united-states-v-paige-thompson?utm_source=openai))



**Current targeting evidence:** Netcraft’s **22 September 2026** report describes US financial-services phishing infrastructure, including a cluster of **16 domains**, **585 unique attack URLs** and **41 impersonated financial brands**. It also reports that a Darcula-based Fidelity impersonation campaign declined to near-zero sites by Q2 2026—evidence that individual campaigns can contract while the broader threat persists. These are phishing observations, not confirmed bank intrusions. [1](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2281754908472386913)



**Plausible pathway:** phishing, vishing, session theft or manipulated recovery → valid SSO/SaaS access → search for sensitive records and credentials → expanded permissions or bulk extraction → extortion or targeted fraud. Alternatively, deception can cause authorised personnel to disclose data without an account compromise.

**Weaknesses:** phishable authentication; weak helpdesk verification; excessive SaaS permissions; ungoverned service accounts; weak validation of legal/government requests; bulk-download monitoring gaps.

**Consequences:** exposure of KYC, credit, wealth and corporate information; customer-protection costs; litigation; targeted follow-on scams. Core banking can remain operational throughout.



Standard Bank’s **14 April 2026** update illustrates that distinction: administrative/document systems were affected, while the bank said transactional and core operating systems remained secure and available. It did not disclose a proven initial-access method. ([standardbank.co.za](https://www.standardbank.co.za/southafrica/news-and-media/newsroom/an-important-data-incident-update-14-april?utm_source=openai))


### 4 — Trusted instructions or endpoints → fraudulent movement of money


**State-linked banking precedent:** DOJ’s Bangladesh Bank case describes compromise of the bank’s local SWIFT processing environment and fraudulent transfers. US authorities attributed the conspiracy to North Korean actors associated with Lazarus; the charging allegations are not convictions. The case demonstrates misuse of a bank-managed payment endpoint—not compromise of SWIFT’s central network. ([justice.gov](https://www.justice.gov/d9/press-releases/attachments/2018/09/06/2018_09_06_park_complaint_unsealed_0.pdf?utm_source=openai))



**Current physical–cyber evidence:** the FBI’s **19 February 2026** bulletin describes ATM attacks exploiting physical and software weaknesses and deploying malware, including Ploutus, to dispense cash without legitimate transactions. It reports more than **700 incidents and $20 million in losses during 2025**. These are reported US ATM events, not a global banking intrusion rate. ([fbi.gov](https://www.fbi.gov/file-repository/increase-in-malware-enabled-atm-jackpotting-incidents-across-united-states-021926.pdf?utm_source=openai))



Original research published **8 April 2026**, covering **2025**, identifies infostealers, mobile banking malware and reuse of stolen credentials as important financial-crime mechanisms. It describes families including Grandoreiro, Coyote and GoPix. These frequently target customer devices or banking sessions; their activity must not automatically be counted as penetration of bank networks. [2](https://my.silobreaker.com/v2/document/5_2281372897521958940)



**Distinct pathways:**
1. Bank endpoint compromise → fraudulent payment instruction → laundering/cash-out.
2. Customer or corporate-user compromise → account takeover or payment deception → apparently authorised transfer.
3. Physical ATM access → malware/control of dispensing software → cash removal.

**Weaknesses:** insufficient separation of payment creation and approval; ineffective independent verification; overprivileged payment accounts; weak beneficiary-change controls; inadequate endpoint protection or physical hardening; poor anomaly detection.

**Consequences:** direct theft, recalls, suspended payment channels, customer disputes and reconciliation demands. A successful cash-out does not require a bank-wide outage.

**Motivations:** organised crime seeks cash; state-linked financially motivated actors can seek revenue. No common actor is inferred across these pathways.


### 5 — DDoS and coordinated disruption → loss of access and processing capacity


Akamai’s **20 May 2026** original report identifies financial services as the most targeted industry in its Layers 3–4 DDoS observations and reports median attack duration up **738% since 2024**. Its report describes pro-Iran hacktivist targeting of banking, payments and APIs. This establishes vendor-observed activity and persistence, not the probability that a particular bank will suffer an outage. ([akamai.com](https://www.akamai.com/newsroom/press-release/financial-services-at-risk-ddos-attacks-are-bigger-longer-and-more-complex-akamai-research-finds?utm_source=openai))



**Plausible pathway:** botnet traffic overwhelms network, application, API or supporting DNS/authentication capacity → login/payment failures and queues → increased customer-support load → degraded service and potentially missed processing windows.

**Weaknesses:** insufficient application-layer mitigation; incomplete API inventories; fragile rate limits; common DNS/network dependencies; untested failover; recovery plans assuming the internet channel remains available.

**Consequences:** digital-channel unavailability and payment delays. Persistent disruption could affect corporate liquidity management; severe sector-wide effects require additional concentration or infrastructure failures and are not implied by every DDoS campaign.

**Actors:** hacktivists seek visibility or geopolitical disruption; criminals may use denial of service for extortion. A group’s claim of responsibility does not independently confirm the claimed outage or motive.


## 3. Evidence-supported control weakness matrix

| Control area | Documented finding | Implication and evidential boundary |
|---|---|---|
| **MFA and account recovery** | Official Scattered Spider guidance documents phishing, push bombing, SIM swapping and recovery manipulation despite MFA. ([content.govdelivery.com](https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/USDHSCISA/bulletins/3eb9139?utm_source=openai)) | **MFA bypass/reset abuse is not absent MFA.** Phishing-resistant authentication must be paired with strong recovery and enrolment controls. No sector-wide MFA absence is established. |
| **Privileged access** | River disabled affected administrative accounts; Singapore’s July 2025 advisory describes identity-to-SaaS and virtualisation access in observed Scattered Spider operations. ([sec.gov](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1641601/000119312526282946/ck0001641601-20260619.htm)) | Compromised administrative capability can expand impact. River’s containment action does not establish how those accounts were first compromised. |
| **Cloud/application configuration** | Capital One’s misconfigured WAF and cloud-risk governance deficiencies are documented. ([justice.gov](https://www.justice.gov/usao-wdwa/united-states-v-paige-thompson?utm_source=openai)) | Historical evidence supports configuration and permission risk; it does not establish the same weakness at current victims. |
| **Remote services and patching** | Official guidance recommends restricting remote access, patching internet-facing systems and removing unnecessary exposure. ([ic3.gov](https://www.ic3.gov/CSA/2025/250729.pdf?utm_source=openai)) | Relevant attack surfaces include VPNs, remote administration and public applications. **No named CVE is assigned to the recent bank incidents without proof of exploitation.** |
| **Third-party access and concentration** | ION demonstrates processing dependence; ECB findings identify shortcomings in critical-function contracts. ([home.treasury.gov](https://home.treasury.gov/system/files/261/FSOC2023AnnualReport.pdf)) | Require restricted access and demonstrable alternatives. Contract non-compliance alone is not proof that remote access was exploited. |
| **Detection and logging** | Singapore’s advisory documents legitimate-tool abuse, credential discovery and suppression of account-activity notifications. ([csa.gov.sg](https://www.csa.gov.sg/alerts-and-advisories/alerts/al-2025-066/)) | Endpoint alerts alone may miss cloud, identity or management-plane abuse. Detection gaps at a particular bank remain unproven unless disclosed. |
| **Backup and recovery** | The ECB exercise identified room for improvement in response and recovery. ([bankingsupervision.europa.eu](https://www.bankingsupervision.europa.eu/press/pr/date/2024/html/ssm.pr240726~06d5776a02.hu.html?utm_source=openai)) | Test isolated restoration, trustworthy records and backlog clearance. Do not infer backup destruction from ransomware or extended downtime. |
| **Payment controls and ATM hardening** | Bangladesh demonstrates payment-endpoint misuse; the FBI documents physical/software ATM compromise. ([justice.gov](https://www.justice.gov/d9/press-releases/attachments/2018/09/06/2018_09_06_park_complaint_unsealed_0.pdf?utm_source=openai)) | Payment legitimacy needs controls beyond authentication; ATM protection must cover physical access and dispensing software. |
| **IT/OT segmentation** | No confirmed bank-facilities OT compromise or sector-wide segmentation failure is established in this investigation. | Facilities controls, power and cooling remain conditional amplification paths. They should not be described as observed bank attack routes. |

## 5. Threat direction and intelligence confidence


**Latest intelligence:** Canada’s Cyber Centre’s **26 October 2026** bulletin describes agentic activity linking reconnaissance, password testing and cloud privilege acquisition. It reports some target-to-privileged-access sequences under three hours, while stressing that humans still direct operations. This is cross-sector intelligence—not proof that the named banking incidents used that framework. ([cyber.gc.ca](https://www.cyber.gc.ca/en/guidance/cyber-threat-bulletin-agentic-artificial-intelligence-accelerating-cyber-attacks?utm_source=openai))



**Latest UK perception evidence:** the Bank of England/FCA survey published **26 October 2026** found **44% of respondents** selected faster exploitation of existing software vulnerabilities as the greatest current frontier-AI cyber channel. This measures respondents’ judgments, not observed exploitation frequency. ([bankofengland.co.uk](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/report/2026/artificial-intelligence-in-uk-financial-services-2026))



**Direction assessment:** faster attack execution and reusable identity/supplier pathways increase the importance of rapid containment and independently recoverable operations. However, current evidence does not justify saying that AI caused every recent attack, that ransomware always destroys backups, or that all financial subsectors share the same exposure.

- **High confidence:** the five scenarios are material; identity, processing suppliers and payment integrity are central dependencies.
- **Moderate confidence:** relative prioritisation for an individual bank, future AI-enabled acceleration and severe cascading effects.
- **Low or unresolved confidence:** undisclosed initial-access methods, precise CVEs, actor claims without corroboration, worldwide frequency changes and estimated financial losses.
- **Key geographic limitation:** detailed primary evidence is weighted toward US and European institutions and supervisors. Worldwide relevance of a mechanism is not evidence of identical prevalence worldwide.

**Credit-intelligence conclusion:** the strongest differentiator is whether a bank can contain compromised identities or suppliers, preserve trustworthy transaction records, and continue minimum payment and customer services. Control ownership, tested recovery and real processing capacity matter more than the existence of policies, MFA or backups in isolation.
